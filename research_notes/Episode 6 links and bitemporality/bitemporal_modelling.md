# Bitemporal data modelling and its gotchas, for retrieval over a versioned document archive

Scope note: this file covers the modelling vocabulary (valid/transaction/decision time), what SQL:2011 standardises and omits, how real systems establish a total order when timestamps tie or are coarse, timestamp-vs-sequence as an ordering key, out-of-order and backdated corrections, documented "as of" query pitfalls, and the (thin) literature on time-scoped retrieval over versioned corpora. Emphasis throughout is on **coarse (day-granular) and tied timestamps**, per the assignment.

Honest framing up front: the *definitional* material is well covered by primary sources (Snodgrass/Dyreson, SQL:2011 summaries, Fowler, vendor docs). The *tie-breaking* material is much weaker — almost nothing in the temporal-database canon tells you what to do when two facts share a day. The strongest material on ties comes from event sourcing, version control and distributed systems, plus one line of Snodgrass/Dyreson work (valid-time indeterminacy) that is the closest thing to a principled treatment of coarse granularity.

## Standard definitions and distinctions: valid time, transaction time, decision time, bitemporal tables; what SQL:2011 standardises and omits

### Takeaway
Valid time (when a fact holds in the modelled world) and transaction time (when the database recorded it) are the two settled axes; "bitemporal" means both, and SQL:2011 standardises them as *application-time period tables* and *system-versioned tables* respectively, with the combination called a "system-versioned application-time period table". Decision time is a real but non-standard third axis, explicitly criticised as application-specific. SQL:2011 deliberately ships periods as two ordinary columns rather than a period *type*, and leaves out coalescing, composability of periods through views/subqueries, outer joins and trigger interaction — all of which matter to anyone building retrieval on top.

### Cited Findings
- SQL:2011 separates transaction time from valid time: *system-versioned tables* handle transaction time, *application-time period tables* handle valid time — [Kulkarni & Michels, "Temporal features in SQL:2011", SIGMOD Record 41(3)](https://old.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/file/Temporal%20features%20in%20SQL2011.pdf); [SQL:2011 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL:2011)
- The system period must be named `SYSTEM_TIME`; an application period may take any user-chosen name; a table may have **at most one** application-time period and **at most one** system-time period — [Kulkarni & Michels, SIGMOD Record](https://old.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/file/Temporal%20features%20in%20SQL2011.pdf)
- A period in SQL:2011 is stored as **two ordinary columns, not a new data type**, which was a deliberate choice easing migration from existing schemas — [Kulkarni & Michels, SIGMOD Record](https://old.dbs.uni-leipzig.de/file/Temporal%20features%20in%20SQL2011.pdf)
- "System-versioned application-time period tables" are what the standard calls the combination, i.e. bitemporal tables; these are the lineal descendants of TSQL2/SQL3 Part 7 ("SQL/Temporal"), which became Part 7 of SQL3 in 1995 and was **cancelled in 2001** because of ISO committee disagreement, its concepts later folded into SQL:2011 — [Snodgrass, "SQL/Temporal" project page, University of Arizona](https://rts.cs.arizona.edu/sql3.html)
- Per that same page: application-time period tables support *sequenced* primary and foreign keys, sequenced single-table insert/delete/update, and *nonsequenced* queries; system-versioned tables support transaction-time **current** keys, current modifications, and current and nonsequenced queries — [Snodgrass, Arizona](https://rts.cs.arizona.edu/sql3.html)
- Syntax, per a hands-on survey: `WITH SYSTEM VERSIONING` plus `PERIOD FOR SYSTEM_TIME` over generated `ROW START`/`ROW END` columns; application periods declared as e.g. `PERIOD FOR valid_at (valid_from, valid_til)` with **source columns required `NOT NULL`**; `FOR PORTION OF valid_at FROM t1 TO t2` for temporal UPDATE/DELETE; temporal primary keys via `WITHOUT OVERLAPS`; period predicates (overlaps, contains, precedes) — [Paul A. Jungwirth, "Survey of SQL:2011 Temporal Features"](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/)
- Documented omissions/weaknesses in the standard, same survey: no **coalescing** (Snodgrass proposed it; the standard does not mention it); **no temporal outer join** (only an inner join via `OVERLAPS`); the standard is **silent on trigger interaction** with temporal DML and says nothing special about `MERGE` for valid-time tables; and **periods are not part of result sets**, so views, subqueries, CTEs and set-returning functions are hard to use with them — [Jungwirth](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/)
- Vendor coverage is uneven: MariaDB has system time (10.3.4+) and application time (10.4.3+) but no temporal PK/FK; DB2 11.5 supports application time **only if the period is named `BUSINESS_TIME`** and does implement temporal PKs; Oracle 19c has application time (via `AS OF PERIOD FOR`) but no `FOR PORTION OF` and no system versioning (Flashback instead); SQL Server 2016/2017+ has system time but **not** application time — [Jungwirth](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/); [SQL:2011 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL:2011)
- PostgreSQL has no in-core SQL:2011 temporal tables; the `temporal_tables` extension does not follow the SQL:2011 design — [SQL:2011 (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SQL:2011)
- Fowler renames the two axes for clarity: **actual history** "records what history should be given perfect transmission of information", **record history** "captures how our knowledge of history changes". He explicitly dropped "valid/effective" and "transaction" because readers found them confusing — [Martin Fowler, "Bitemporal History"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- **Decision time** is a third axis: "the time when a decision was made about a fact stored in a database", used to track how decisions about valid times changed over time; a database with all three is *tri-temporal*. The term is attributed to Mario Nascimento and Margaret Eich (1995) — [Decision time (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_time) (note: the definition there carries a "citation needed" tag)
- Decision time is treated sceptically in the research literature: the number and meaning of "the decision time(s)" of a fact varies from application to application, making it far less standardised than transaction time; transaction time is described as the most-used temporal functionality — [Christian S. Jensen, thesis chapter 1, Aalborg University](https://homes.cs.aau.dk/~csj/Thesis/pdf/chapter1.pdf) (sourced via search result summary; **the PDF itself refused connection when I tried to fetch it**, so treat the wording as paraphrase, not quotation); corroborated by [Temporal database (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_database)
- Fowler & Carlson & Estepp's earlier PLoP '98 "Temporal Patterns" distinguishes a **Temporality** pattern (handles only actual time; record time is assumed to be always *now*) from a **Bi-Temporality** pattern (the client may specify a record time) — [Fowler, "Temporal Patterns"](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/timeNarrative.html); [PLoP '98 paper](https://hillside.net/plop/plop98/final_submissions/P09.pdf)
- Fowler's **Temporal Property** pattern frames the API as a map keyed by date: "provide a get and put operation that uses a date as an index" — [Fowler, "Temporal Property"](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/TemporalProperty.html)

### Inferences
- For a retrieval system the asymmetry between the two axes matters more than the symmetry: transaction/record time is append-only and bounded by *now*, whereas valid/actual time is editable and may extend into the past or the future. Fowler makes this explicit for record history ("We don't change what we thought we knew about Sally's salary on Feb 25") — so an index keyed on transaction time can be built incrementally and never invalidated, while an index keyed on valid time can be rewritten by a backdated correction.
- The SQL:2011 "periods are two plain columns, not a type" decision is why period semantics leak: because the period is not a first-class value, it cannot flow through a view or subquery, which is exactly the composability complaint in Jungwirth's survey. A retrieval layer that wants to carry validity through a scoring pipeline therefore has to model the interval itself rather than borrow the database's.
- Decision time is probably the right *name* for the thing a document archive actually has when a spec says "changed on 2024-03-01 to say it had been true since 2024-01-01" and the change was *agreed* at some third moment — but given its non-standard status, borrowing the name buys vocabulary, not tooling.

### Gaps
- I could not open Jensen's thesis chapter (connection refused) nor extract text from Snodgrass's book PDF (see next section), so the strongest *primary* definitional sources are cited second-hand here. The canonical primary citation for the vocabulary is Jensen & Dyreson (eds.), "A Consensus Glossary of Temporal Database Concepts — February 1998 Version", which I did not retrieve in full.
- The actual text of SQL:2011 is paywalled; all claims about it here come from Kulkarni & Michels (two of the standard's editors, so near-primary), the Arizona project page, and a practitioner survey.

## Establishing a total order when timestamps tie or are only day-granular

### Takeaway
No temporal-database standard supplies a tie-breaking rule; every system that needs a total order introduces a **second, non-temporal key** — a transaction id, a per-stream version number, a branch sequence number, a command id, or a (Lamport counter, node id) pair — and treats the timestamp as a label rather than a key. The temporal-database literature's own answer to coarse timestamps is different and more honest: Snodgrass and Dyreson model a coarse time as *indeterminate*, i.e. a set of possible finer instants, and make queries take an explicit credibility/plausibility parameter rather than pretending an order exists.

### Cited Findings
- **Datomic** is explicit that the ordering key is the transaction id, not the instant: "Every transaction is assigned a transaction id that is unique within the database. Transactions are fully serialized, and transaction ids increase over time, so transaction ids are a total ordering of all transactions." Wall-clock time is carried *separately* as `:db/txInstant` ("Datomic assigns a `:db/txInstant` for every transaction, so every datom knows the wall clock time it was added") — [Datomic data model](https://docs.datomic.com/data-model.html); [Datomic ACID](https://docs.datomic.com/transactions/acid.html)
- Datomic's `t`/basis is described as "a global ordering of transactions for a particular system. Peers always see all transactions up to their time basis, in order, with no gaps" — [Datomic ACID](https://docs.datomic.com/transactions/acid.html)
- Datomic's log API accepts *either* kind of key interchangeably — "Txes (transaction entity ids), Ts, Transaction instants (java.util.Dates), Nil" — which is the clearest illustration that instants are a *lookup convenience* layered over an id order — [Datomic log reference](https://docs.datomic.com/reference/log.html)
- A Datomic forum thread reports that **history-database indexes sort by the assert/retract boolean before `T`**, so `T` values are not ordered on their own in that index, and maintainers said no ordering by that flag is guaranteed — [Datomic forum](https://forum.datomic.com/t/history-db-indexes-sorted-by-op-t/770). This is a concrete "ordering you assumed isn't the ordering you get" failure mode.
- **XTDB**: official docs say transactions are processed serially and the system timestamp comes from a single-writer Write-Ahead Log, and that system-time columns cannot be modified — [XTDB data model](https://docs.xtdb.com/intro/data-model). On ties, the only statement I found is a maintainer in the Clojurians `#xtdb` channel (2022): XT is a single-threaded writer; **transaction time may not strictly increase, since two consecutive transactions can share a timestamp; ties are broken by transaction id**, which the transaction log assigns and which increases monotonically, and that is what produces the total order — [Clojurians log, 2022-11-18](https://clojurians-log.clojureverse.org/xtdb/2022-11-18). **Caveat: this is a chat log, not documentation, and is three years old.** XTDB v1 docs do state the related constraint that you cannot write a new transaction with a transaction time in the past — [XTDB v1 bitemporality](https://v1-docs.xtdb.com/concepts/bitemporality/)
- **Version control as a worked precedent**: Launchpad/Bazaar found that "clock problems and merged revisions mean the revision date is not always in order", and therefore query by **branch sequence number** instead of date — [Launchpad bug 742916 comment](https://bugs.qastaging.launchpad.net/launchpad/+bug/742916/comments/5). Relatedly, "Subversion requires a total ordering of commits, whereas git only requires a topological ordering" — [PostgreSQL -hackers list](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/4C753960.5000501%40alum.mit.edu)
- **Lamport-style ties**: COPS uses Lamport timestamps as versions, and "when combined with a last-writer-wins tie-breaking mechanism, this yields a global ordering for all writes to a key" — i.e. the Lamport counter is paired with a node number to total-order it (via search summary of [arXiv consensus paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.03319)). A TLA+ consensus spec compares timestamps and, **if they match, breaks the tie by comparing command ids** — [same](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.03319). A practitioner design sets each new counter above everything the node has seen and appends a cluster-wide unique tie-breaker — [PostgreSQL -hackers, "Option to ensure monotonic timestamps"](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/CAH8WVsixOeUOb%3DXdfy6%2Bkq0w0zkHLSnNZ1100tj6xRhAPaz%2BBw%40mail.gmail.com)
- That PostgreSQL thread also isolates the weaker-but-sufficient property most systems actually want: the proposer "did not need timestamps to be unique, only correctly ordered when they differ" — equal timestamps are acceptable, but unequal timestamps must not be mis-ordered — [PostgreSQL -hackers](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/CAH8WVsixOeUOb%3DXdfy6%2Bkq0w0zkHLSnNZ1100tj6xRhAPaz%2BBw%40mail.gmail.com)
- **The temporal-database answer to coarseness**: Dyreson & Snodgrass model *valid-time indeterminacy* — events known to have happened but whose exact occurrence time is unknown — representing the occurrence time as a **set of possible instants with a probability distribution over them** — [Dyreson & Snodgrass, "Supporting Valid-Time Indeterminacy", ACM TODS 23(1), 1998](https://ulp.cs.arizona.edu/people/rts/pubs/TODS98.pdf) (note: the `smsf.cs.arizona.edu` mirror returned **HTTP 403** when I fetched it; cited from search metadata and the ICDE'93 precursor [ICDE 1993](https://ulp.cs.arizona.edu/people/rts/pubs/ICDE93p335.pdf))
- The crucial claim for a day-granular corpus: **granularity is the unit of measure of a temporal value, and a time recorded at one granularity is indeterminate at every finer granularity** — [Dyreson dissertation, University of Arizona](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/186920); [Dyreson, granularity notes](https://www.usu.edu/cs/people/CurtisDyreson/temporal/granularity.htm) (the USU page now **307-redirects to a faculty directory page**; content cited from search summary only)
- Dyreson's dissertation introduces the *indeterminate instant* data type over a contiguous set of chronons, reports that the performance cost of supporting indeterminacy is modest, and adds a "now-relative" indeterminate instant — [Dyreson dissertation](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/186920)
- A formal model unifying granularity with indeterminacy, extending SQL-92 with `scale` and `cast` operations to move times between granularities (e.g. days to months): Dyreson, Evans, Lin & Snodgrass, "Efficiently Supporting Temporal Granularities", IEEE TKDE, July 2000 — [PDF](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/~rts/pubs/TKDEJuly00.pdf)
- **A documented self-reinforcing failure mode** when timestamps are used to *infer* sequence: a Camunda issue records that inferring sequence from timestamps and then reinforcing those guesses biases future ordering, leading the team to consider excluding ambiguous events from sequence counts entirely — [Camunda OPT-3609](https://jira.camunda.com/browse/OPT-3609)

### Inferences
- The options reduce to three, with distinct failure modes:
  1. **Exogenous sequence (transaction id, per-stream version, branch revno)** — the only option that yields a genuine total order. Failure mode: the sequence is *storage* order, not *authored* order, so if the archive was loaded out of order the sequence encodes ingestion, not the document's own history. Datomic/XTDB get away with it because the writer *is* the authority on order; a document archive ingested from files has no such authority.
  2. **Timestamp + deterministic secondary key (node id, command id, document order, file order, hash)** — cheap and reproducible. Failure mode: it is *arbitrary*, so it will be silently wrong roughly half the time on a same-day pair, and because it is deterministic the wrongness is stable and therefore invisible in regression tests.
  3. **Admit indeterminacy (Snodgrass/Dyreson)** — model the day as a set of possible instants and let a query return "possibly" as well as "definitely" matching revisions, with an explicit credibility/plausibility knob. Failure mode: it changes the contract — a retrieval API must then return a *set* or a ranked pair rather than "the" revision.
- Given a corpus where 50% of multi-change specs have same-day collisions, option 2's arbitrariness is not a rounding error; it is a coin flip on half the hard cases. That argues for either (a) finding an exogenous intra-day ordinal in the source documents themselves (amendment number, section order, revision label, changelog position — the document-order option), or (b) adopting option 3's honesty and surfacing the ambiguity to the caller.
- **Document order deserves naming as its own strategy**: within a single spec file, the order in which same-day changes are written down is itself an ordinal, and it is authored by the same humans as the dates. I found no literature evaluating document order as a tie-breaker for temporal data (see Gaps), but it is the analogue of Datomic's "the writer defines the order" — the author, not the ingester, is the authority.
- Lamport clocks are the wrong tool here and should be ruled out explicitly: they order *causally related* events in a distributed system. A document archive has no concurrency and no message passing, so there is no happens-before relation to recover; a Lamport counter over ingestion would just be option 1 under a grander name.

### Gaps
- **No source in the temporal-database canon prescribes a tie-breaking rule for equal valid-time timestamps.** I searched for it directly and found only the distributed-systems and event-sourcing material above. This is a genuine hole in the literature, and worth stating as such in the report rather than papering over.
- I could not retrieve the full text of the Dyreson/Snodgrass indeterminacy papers (403 on one mirror; the USU topic pages now redirect away). The claims cited are from abstracts, search summaries and the dissertation record. Anyone relying on the credibility/plausibility semantics should read TODS 23(1) 1998 directly.
- Snodgrass, *Developing Time-Oriented Database Applications in SQL* (Morgan Kaufmann, 1999) is downloadable at [https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/~rts/tdbbook.pdf](https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/~rts/tdbbook.pdf) and I confirmed the file exists (~4.8MB), but **it is a scanned/image PDF** (CCITTFax and DCT-encoded page images) with no usable text layer available to me, so I could not quote it. Chapter 12 is noted as containing an extensive comparison of standard SQL with the proposed temporal support — [Arizona page](https://rts.cs.arizona.edu/sql3.html). This book is the single best primary source for closed-open period conventions and for the sequenced/nonsequenced distinction, and it remains unquoted here.
- I found **no published measurement** of how often same-day collisions occur in real versioned corpora, so the corpus's own 50% figure has no external benchmark to sit against.

## Ordering by timestamp vs ordering by sequence, and when a timestamp is the wrong key

### Takeaway
The consistent position across event-sourcing and event-store literature is that timestamps are for display and audit while sequence/version numbers are for ordering — because timestamps can collide, can go backwards, cannot distinguish events that genuinely share an instant, and encode no gap information. A timestamp is the wrong key whenever the question is "which came next" rather than "what was true then"; it is the right key only for the latter.

### Cited Findings
- "Timestamps are not reliable for ordering events, because clocks drift, especially across nodes"; a timestamp "is only reliable per device emitting events", so there is no reliable global order from timestamps alone — [Eventsandstuff, "Essential features of an Event Store for Event Sourcing"](https://eventsandstuff.substack.com/p/essential-features-of-an-event-store)
- That same piece argues version numbers belong **in the store, not application code**, since it is not possible to assign them across streams in application logic alone, and that a store needs a **global** ordering across all events in addition to per-stream ordering — [Eventsandstuff](https://eventsandstuff.substack.com/p/essential-features-of-an-event-store)
- Practitioner guidance for webhook/event consumers: prefer revision counters or sequence numbers over timestamps where available — [Hookdeck, "Webhook Ordering: Why It's Hard and How to Handle It"](https://hookdeck.com/webhooks/guides/webhook-ordering-why-its-hard-and-how-to-handle-it)
- A per-aggregate increasing sequence lets consumers **detect gaps** and **discard stale updates** — a capability a timestamp cannot provide — [Gravitee, "Event Ordering in Distributed Systems"](https://gravitee.io/corpus/gen-2119/event-driven-soa/event-ordering.html)
- The git/Subversion contrast is the cleanest statement of the two different *kinds* of order people conflate: a **topological** order (git: enough to know what descends from what) versus a **total** order (Subversion: a single global revision line) — [PostgreSQL -hackers](https://www.postgresql.org/message-id/4C753960.5000501%40alum.mit.edu)

### Inferences
- Two distinct jobs are being asked of one column. "Give me the revision in force on date D" is a **valid-time containment** question and a timestamp (or day) is exactly the right key for it. "Which of these two same-day changes superseded the other" is a **succession** question, and a timestamp of insufficient resolution cannot answer it even in principle — not because clocks are unreliable, but because the information was never recorded. Conflating them is the root error.
- Therefore the useful design rule for a day-granular archive: keep the day as the *selection* key (which revisions are candidates for cutoff D) and introduce a separate ordinal as the *succession* key (which candidate wins). These should be different fields, and the retrieval contract should say which one it used.
- The "detect gaps" property is the under-appreciated argument for sequence numbers in a retrieval setting: with a per-document ordinal you can tell that revision 3 exists but is missing from the index. With dates alone, a missing revision is indistinguishable from no revision.

### Gaps
- All the event-sourcing sources here are blog posts and vendor guides rather than named-author books or papers. I did not find a canonical citation (e.g. Fowler's own event-sourcing writing, or Young/Vernon) that states the timestamp-vs-sequence rule in so many words. Treat this section as well-attested practitioner consensus, not as literature.

## Out-of-order arrival, backdated corrections, retroactive amendment, and the vocabulary for them

### Takeaway
Fowler supplies the clearest working vocabulary — *retroactive change*, *discrepancy*, *perspective* — and the key structural rule: actual (valid) history can be edited, record (transaction) history cannot. Johnston's *Asserted Versioning* supplies the richer industry taxonomy (assertion time, deferred assertions) but I could not retrieve its definitions from primary sources.

### Cited Findings
- Fowler's worked example gives the canonical shape of "changed on X to say it had been true since Y": Jan 25 payroll pays $6000; **Feb 15** is when the raise takes effect; Feb 25 payroll still pays $6000 "because the raise wasn't yet known"; **Mar 15** HR's letter says the Feb 15 raise was $6500; **Apr 5** a correction says it was $6400, not $6500. Fowler: "on February 25 we thought the salary was $6000", and "we never see Sally's $6500 salary because it was never true" — [Fowler, "Bitemporal History"](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- Fowler's terms: a **retroactive change** is a correction to past actual history; a **discrepancy** is a gap between actual and record history; a **perspective** is a named record-time view, e.g. "HR, Feb 15 – Dec 31" or "Payroll, Apr 5 – Dec 31" — [Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- Fowler's structural rule: record history is append-only — "We don't change what we thought we knew about Sally's salary on Feb 25" — while actual history can be edited — [Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- Why retroactive changes hurt: "Retroactive changes are a problem when actions are based on a past state that's retroactively changed" (his example is a cheque issued on the old salary), and "If we've paid Sally $6000 when we should have paid her $6400, then we need to make it right" — downstream effects on thresholds and taxes that bitemporal history alone does not resolve — [Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- Fowler's avoidance advice, worth quoting as a design option: avoid bitemporality by **not allowing retroactive changes**, or by making changes before the action that depends on them — [Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html); a commentator reads him as saying "Bi-temporality is the full solution, but it's always worth thinking of ways around it" — [JUXT, "Bitemporality: more than a design pattern"](https://juxt.pro/blog/bitemporality-more-than-a-design-pattern)
- On storage granularity Fowler is permissive: each event needs "an actual date (or time)" and a record date or time — day-level and time-level precision are both allowed — [Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/articles/bitemporal-history.html)
- Johnston & Weis, *Managing Time in Relational Databases* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2010) presents a taxonomy of bi-temporal data management methods and introduces **Asserted Versioning**, which extends standard bitemporal theory; it contains a chapter "Deferred Assertions and Other Pipeline Datasets". The second edition is Johnston, *Bitemporal Data: Theory and Practice* (2014) — [Elsevier/Morgan Kaufmann listing](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/managing-time-in-relational-databases/johnston/978-0-12-375041-9); [O'Reilly listing](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/managing-time-in/9780123750419/)
- XTDB's transaction-time rule enforces the append-only property at the engine level: you cannot write a new transaction with a transaction time in the past — [XTDB v1 bitemporality](https://v1-docs.xtdb.com/concepts/bitemporality/)

### Inferences
- Fowler's *perspective* is the most directly reusable idea for a retrieval API: a query carries both a record-time perspective ("as known on") and an actual-time point ("as of"), and naming the pair makes the two cutoffs impossible to confuse at a call site. A single `asOf(date)` parameter is the API-level embodiment of the bug.
- The asymmetry (valid time editable, transaction time not) determines where a retrieval index can be incremental. A transaction-time index is append-only and safe to cache; a valid-time index must be invalidated per document whenever a backdated correction lands.
- Johnston's "assertion time" is roughly Fowler's record time with a stronger claim attached (the period during which the database *asserts* the row is true), and "deferred assertion" names a row recorded now but asserted to become true later — the forward-dated analogue of a retroactive change. I state this as inference, not as a sourced definition, because I could not reach Johnston's text.

### Gaps
- **I could not retrieve primary definitions from Johnston's work.** Searches returned only bookseller pages and tables of contents. The precise Asserted Versioning definitions of *assertion time*, *deferred assertion*, *retroactive/proactive/current* updates, and the "episode" concept remain unverified; do not attribute specific wording to Johnston on the strength of this file.
- I found no source documenting what happens when a backdated correction is itself superseded by another backdated correction *on the same day* — the double-collision case that the target corpus will have.

## Documented pitfalls around "as of" queries: bounds, open intervals, timezones, and valid-vs-transaction

### Takeaway
Bound inclusivity is **not** consistent even within a single vendor's own clause set — MariaDB's `FROM...TO` is half-open while its `BETWEEN` is fully inclusive — and "forever" is a per-vendor sentinel rather than a true infinity, with values as low as 2038. All the practical guidance converges on: store UTC, query half-open `[start, end)`, never compute a day's end as start + 24h, and test exactly at the boundary.

### Cited Findings
- **The same engine, two inclusivity rules.** MariaDB: for `FROM start TO end`, results include rows visible "including start, but excluding end"; for `BETWEEN`, it "works inclusively, a row visible exactly at start or exactly at end will be shown too" — [MariaDB, System-Versioned Tables](https://mariadb.com/docs/server/reference/sql-structure/temporal-tables/system-versioned-tables)
- **SQL Server's overlap semantics**, stated in prose rather than symbols: `FROM...TO` and `BETWEEN...AND` "return row versions that overlap with a specified period (that is, those that started before the given period and ended after it), while `CONTAINED IN` returns only those that existed within the specified period boundaries" — [Microsoft Learn, "Query data in a system-versioned temporal table"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/tables/temporal/query-data)
- **UTC is mandatory and silent** in SQL Server: values supplied to `AS OF` "are interpreted as UTC time", and reconstruction precision is that of the `datetime2` used in the `PERIOD` definition — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/tables/temporal/query-data). EF Core's reference warns that because temporal data is stored in UTC, **local-time `DateTime` arguments can produce unexpected results** — [Microsoft Learn, `TemporalAsOf`](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/dotnet/api/microsoft.entityframeworkcore.sqlserverdbsetextensions.temporalasof)
- **"Forever" is a sentinel, and the sentinels differ wildly**: the SQL:2011 example is `3000-01-01`; DB2 uses `9999-12-30-00.00.00.000000000000`; SQL Server uses `9999-12-31 23:59:59.9999999`; MariaDB's `timestamp(6)` maximum is **`2147483647.999999`, displayed as 2038-01-19 05:14:07.999999** — [Jungwirth](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/); [MariaDB](https://mariadb.com/docs/server/reference/sql-structure/temporal-tables/system-versioned-tables)
- **NULL-as-unbounded is non-portable**: the standard requires period source columns be `NOT NULL` and MariaDB enforces it, but Oracle allows NULLs and treats them as unbounded — [Jungwirth](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/)
- **Range queries do not clip bounds**: a time-range system-time query leaves the end column at its sentinel value rather than truncating it to the requested range — so a returned row's period is not the period you asked about — [Jungwirth](https://illuminatedcomputing.com/posts/2019/08/sql2011-survey/)
- **Zero-length row versions are observable**: MariaDB's own documentation shows a row whose `row_start` and `row_end` are both `2033-03-03 00:00:00.000000` after an insert and delete at the same forced timestamp, without explaining it as a general rule. Separately, MariaDB creates a history row "for every `UPDATE` statement that includes a versioned column in its `SET` list", **even when the values do not change** — [MariaDB](https://mariadb.com/docs/server/reference/sql-structure/temporal-tables/system-versioned-tables)
- **Day-boundary guidance**: convert local day boundaries to UTC and then use a half-open interval, which avoids inclusive-end mistakes; do **not** compute the end as start + 24 hours, because DST transitions make some local days 23 or 25 hours long; and test exact midnight, one microsecond before the cutoff, and both DST transitions, because checking only a final total can hide a boundary error — [practitioner guide, DEV Community](https://dev.to/mads_hansen_27b33ebfee4c9/your-ai-does-not-know-what-today-means-neg) (blog-quality source; the advice is corroborated by the vendor docs above but the source itself is not authoritative)
- **Local-time collapse**: conversions between UTC and local zones are not one-to-one, so distinct UTC values can collapse to the same local time during a DST transition — i.e. timezone conversion can *manufacture* ties — [MySQL 8.4 Reference Manual, timestamp lookups](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E17952_01/mysql-8.4-en/timestamp-lookups.html)
- **Aggregation over a window silently multiplies rows**: SQL Server documents that `FOR SYSTEM_TIME BETWEEN ... AND` "returns every row version that overlaps the period", so when a value changed within the window the query returns multiple versions per entity and each contributes to the aggregate — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/tables/temporal/query-data)
- `FOR SYSTEM_TIME` can be specified **independently per table** in a query, and applying `AS OF` to a view propagates it to all temporal tables in that view but not to non-temporal ones; a view referencing no temporal table fails outright — a documented source of "half the query time-travelled" bugs — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/sql/relational-databases/tables/temporal/query-data)

### Inferences
- With **day granularity**, the inclusive/exclusive choice is not cosmetic: a revision whose validity begins on day D is either in or out of an "as of D" query depending on the convention, and because the convention differs between `FROM...TO` and `BETWEEN` *in the same product*, any system that layers its own semantics over a database's will inherit whichever the implementer happened to use. The only safe discipline is to define the archive's own convention explicitly (closed-open `[start, end)`, start-inclusive) and to test exactly at the boundary day, not a day either side.
- A day-granular corpus has a second, subtler version of the MySQL finding: *choosing* a timezone can create same-day collisions that do not exist in the source data, or split a single authored day into two. If source dates are bare dates with no zone, treating them as UTC midnights is a decision to document, not a detail.
- "As of" (valid) and "as known" (transaction) need distinct words in the API, and the vendor evidence shows why: SQL Server's `FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF` means *as known at*, whereas Oracle's `AS OF PERIOD FOR valid_at` means *as valid at*. The identical phrase "AS OF" denotes opposite axes in two mainstream products. Any prose or parameter name using "as of" unqualified is ambiguous by construction.
- The MariaDB 2038 sentinel is a reminder not to encode "still in force" as a far-future date in application code. An explicit open end (null, or a dedicated flag) is more honest, at the cost of needing `COALESCE`-style handling in every comparison.

### Gaps
- I found no authoritative, standards-level statement of whether SQL:2011 periods are closed-open; Jungwirth's survey explicitly notes the question is unaddressed in his material, and the primary text is paywalled. Snodgrass's book is the place this is settled, and I could not extract its text (scanned PDF).
- I found no documented account of how any production temporal system handles an `AS OF` query whose cutoff falls on a day with multiple changes — i.e. exactly the target corpus's situation. The pitfall literature covers boundary *inclusivity* thoroughly and *intra-period multiplicity* not at all.

## Patterns for retrieval and search systems answering time-scoped queries over versioned corpora

### Takeaway
There is a real but small literature: Berberich, Bedathur, Neumann and Weikum's "time-travel text search" (2007) is the foundational work, attaching half-open validity intervals to individual inverted-index postings so a keyword query can be evaluated against the collection state as of a time point; the main recent work (SIGIR-AP 2024) adds reproducibility by logging the query's execution timestamp and a result-set hash. Neither addresses timestamp granularity or ties.

### Cited Findings
- **The foundational system**: Klaus Berberich, Srikanta Bedathur, Thomas Neumann and Gerhard Weikum, "FluxCapacitor: Efficient Time-Travel Text Search", VLDB '07, Vienna — [VLDB 2007 demo paper](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf). The problem statement is that text search over temporally versioned collections such as web archives "has received little attention", leaving no scalable, principled way to search a collection as of a specified time — [MPI technical report](https://domino.mpi-inf.mpg.de/internet/reports.nsf/9cd2c99e39f4ea47c1256e890051b300/bd9f07c1e72cd3d7c125731b0049a242/$FILE/MPI-2007-5-002.pdf)
- **The index design**: each posting carries a validity interval written **`[t_b, t_e)`** — explicitly half-open — with postings of the form `(d; p; [t_b, t_e))`. Consequently there is **one posting per version of each document**, not one per document, and the index grows substantially: the authors estimate **more than 257 GB** for a temporally extended index over English Wikipedia — [VLDB 2007](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- **Query model**: a time-travel query pairs a keyword query with a time point `t`, evaluated against the collection state as of `t`. Scoring builds on BM25; the authors note that **idf-related scores are time-varying** in this setting and are handled separately from tf scores — [VLDB 2007](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- **Approximate temporal coalescing**: because most document versions change only slightly, temporally adjacent postings for a document often have nearly equal payloads; these are merged subject to a maximum error bound ε. This reduces to finding a compact piecewise-constant representation of a time series (connected to prior work on optimal and relative-error-bounded histograms), solved in linear time near-optimally. In their example, coalescing cuts postings from 9 to 3; experiments indicate even small non-zero ε shrinks the index substantially while **barely affecting top-10 and top-100 results** — [VLDB 2007](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- **The modern reproducibility work**: Moritz Staudinger, Florina Piroi and Andreas Rauber (TU Wien), "Reproducible Hybrid Time-Travel Retrieval in Evolving Corpora", SIGIR-AP '24, Tokyo — [arXiv:2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04051v1). Documents carry `valid_from`/`valid_to` in a MonetDB table so the system can reconstruct which versions existed at a given time; when Lucene overwrites an updated document, old terms are marked deleted with a deletion timestamp and a new entry is created — [same](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04051v1)
- **Their as-of contract**: every executed query is logged with a persistent identifier, its execution timestamp, a hash of the result set, and the result count. Resolving the identifier re-executes the stored query against MonetDB **using the original execution timestamp as the filter**; the BM25 SQL keeps only versions valid at that timestamp and recomputes collection statistics (document count, average length, document frequency) **as they stood then**. The re-computed result is checked against the stored hash; absent a timestamp, the most recent index state is queried — [arXiv:2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04051v1)
- **A near-tie failure mode they actually measured**: near-identical scores arising from floating-point differences between Lucene and MonetDB occasionally return in swapped order — about **1 in 10,000** in their observations — detected via the hash mismatch and corrected by swapping the candidate pair — [arXiv:2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04051v1)
- **Follow-on work**: a 2011 Università di Padova thesis applies versioned indexing to Terrier with "Baseline" and "Mapping" approaches, evaluated on CLEF and Wikipedia data — [thesis PDF](https://thesis.unipd.it/retrieve/91bb7b3b-dd34-4f26-9f3d-8a758bd028d0/Tesi_Andrea.pdf); Berberich's dissertation covers the area at length — [Saarland University](https://jahrbib.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/26052/1/Dissertation_159_Berb_Klau_2010.pdf)

### Inferences
- The time-travel IR literature answers a *different* question from the one the target system faces. It assumes versions are plentiful, cheap and automatically timestamped at fine resolution (web crawls, wiki edits) and worries about index size — hence coalescing. A day-granular spec archive has the opposite profile: few versions, coarse timestamps, and high per-revision semantic weight. Coalescing is therefore the wrong borrowing; the *half-open posting interval* and the *recompute-statistics-as-of-t* discipline are the right ones.
- The 2024 paper's reproducibility mechanism is the most transferable idea: pin the retrieval to a recorded timestamp *and* hash the result set, so that a later run that silently resolves a tie differently is **detected** rather than tolerated. Applied to same-day collisions, a result-set hash turns an invisible coin flip into a test failure.
- Berberich et al.'s note that **idf is time-varying** is an underappreciated correctness trap for any time-scoped retrieval: if collection statistics are computed over the whole corpus while postings are filtered to time `t`, scores are not the scores the as-of corpus would have produced. The 2024 paper treats recomputing document count, average length and document frequency as of `t` as part of the contract.
- Neither paper distinguishes valid from transaction time — the 2024 paper's `valid_from`/`valid_to` is the closest analogue to valid time and it never records when a change was learned. So the IR literature is effectively **uni-temporal**: there is no published retrieval system I found that answers a genuinely bitemporal query ("as of valid date D, as known on record date R") over a text corpus.

### Gaps
- **No retrieval-system source addresses coarse or tied timestamps.** Both the VLDB 2007 and SIGIR-AP 2024 papers are silent on granularity; the 2024 paper does not even state whether its timestamps are seconds or milliseconds. The near-tie it does report is a *score* tie, not a *time* tie.
- I found no published work on bitemporal (two-axis) retrieval over text, and no evaluation benchmark for "retrieve the correct revision given a cutoff". If one exists it did not surface in these searches.
- The newest work I found is December 2024; I found nothing covering retrieval-augmented generation over versioned corpora with temporal cutoffs, which is the obvious adjacent application. Searches for it returned the same 2007/2024 cluster.
