# The COMPOSITE query pattern: resolve a set, extract a judgement per member, synthesise a consensus

Driving example: *"give me the consensus current outlook of all the companies with exposure to company Z."*

Stages: (1) resolve Z; (2) find **all** companies with exposure to Z (set-valued, completeness-bound); (3) extract each member's "current outlook" from prose; (4) synthesise a "consensus".

Metric hygiene used throughout. **[RET]** = retrieval metric (Recall@k, Hit@k, nDCG, MRR). **[EXT]** = extraction metric (precision/recall/F1 over relations or labels, audit confirmation rate). **[ANS]** = answer or judge metric (LLM-judge pairwise preference, Exact Match, citation precision/recall via NLI, rubric score). These are never interchangeable and the literature frequently mixes them.

## Q1. The ambiguity in "exposure": financial vs operational, and which is genuinely latent

### Takeaway
The phrase splits into two populations with disjoint machinery: *financial* exposure (institutional holders of Z) is already a structured, enumerable table, so it is a database query and needs no graph and no LLM; *operational* exposure (revenue/supply/operations depending on Z) is the genuinely latent case, because the counterparty's identity is only sometimes named and the dependence is asserted in prose. Only the operational reading makes the COMPOSITE pattern a retrieval problem at all.

### Cited Findings
- Holder-side exposure is a structured product, not a latent relation. Commercial and academic ownership datasets are built from the 13F holdings tables as rows (CUSIP, shares, value), and are distributed as relational tables through WRDS-style providers; the supply-chain vendors are explicitly described as a *separate* product covering "customer, supplier, and competitive relationship" data — [Penn Libraries FAQ on supply-chain relationship sources](https://faq.library.upenn.edu/business/faq/45376)
- The operational relation is declared only under conditions. US GAAP segment reporting requires an entity to disclose *that* a ≥10%-of-revenue customer exists, the amount of revenue from it, and the segment reporting it — and explicitly states the entity "need not disclose the identity of a major customer or the amount of revenues that each segment reports from that customer" — [Deloitte DART, ASC 280 Roadmap §5.7 "Information About Major Customers" (ASC 280-10-50-42)](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc280-10/roadmap-segment-reporting/chapter-5-entity-wide-disclosures/5-7-information-about-major-customers)
- Where a name *is* given voluntarily in the notes, it still has to comply with ASC 280-10-50-42; SEC staff have told registrants that electing to identify major customers does not escape the standard — [Deloitte DART §5.7](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc280-10/roadmap-segment-reporting/chapter-5-entity-wide-disclosures/5-7-information-about-major-customers)
- The one paper in this space that frames the problem the same way the series does calls these links **latent edges**: standard sector classifications such as GICS miss economic links between firms — supplier, customer, IP licensing, cloud, power-purchase — which cross sector lines and "appear only in unstructured text" — [LLM Latent Edge Measurement: Point-in-Time Economic Graphs for Quantitative Investing from Corporate Disclosures, arXiv:2607.15640, submitted 17 Jul 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15640)
- Vendor coverage confirms the asymmetry in effort: FactSet's supply-chain database holds "over 150,000 relationships", reviewed annually and built from SEC filings, investor presentations and press releases — i.e. a vendor pays humans to read prose precisely because no structured field holds it — [FactSet Insight, Revere industry classification / supply chain / hierarchy data](https://insight.factset.com/category-tools-and-tips-factset-reveres-industry-classification-supply-chain-and-hierarchy-data-can-now-be-used-in-your-templates-and-custom-reports?hs_amp=true); [Harvard Business School Library, FactSet Revere Supply Chain Relationships](https://www.library.hbs.edu/find/databases/factset-revere-supply-chain-relationships)

### Inferences
- The project's earlier conclusion — *factoid retrieval over a corpus of declared links does not need a graph* — survives intact for the financial reading. 13F holder sets are declared links in tabular form; the correct answer there is `SELECT`, and a graph adds nothing.
- The operational reading is the first candidate in this project for a relation that is **undeclared, directional, and asymmetric**: Z's own filing may never mention the dependent company, because the disclosure obligation sits on the *dependent* side (the firm with concentration risk), not on Z. That asymmetry is what makes stage 2 a recall problem rather than a lookup.
- Because the relation is asserted on the dependent side, "all companies with exposure to Z" cannot be answered by retrieving Z's documents at all. This is a structural argument for stage 2 being a different shape of problem, independent of whether a graph is the right answer.

### Gaps
- No source found that quantifies what fraction of real customer-concentration disclosures actually name the counterparty versus describing it as "one customer" or "a significant customer". This is the single most decision-relevant missing number for stage 2 and the series would have to measure it on its own corpus (without crawling sec.gov — a pre-built filings dataset would be needed).

## Q2. Operational exposure: the disclosure rule, and the state of supply-chain / customer-concentration extraction

### Takeaway
There are two separate 10% rules and they differ on naming: the accounting standard (ASC 280-10-50-42) requires the *existence* and *amount* but explicitly not the name, while Regulation S-K Item 101 historically required the *name* when a customer was ≥10% of revenue **and** losing it would be materially adverse — a two-part test whose current paragraph numbering and post-2020 survival I could not verify from primary rule text. On extraction, the published work is thin, recent, and reports audit-style confirmation rates rather than true precision/recall against a complete gold standard; every paper that touches completeness says its graph is not complete.

### Cited Findings — the rule and the threshold
- The naming test as consistently described by registrants and SEC staff in comment-letter correspondence: the name of a customer and its relationship with the registrant must be disclosed where sales to that customer are ≥10% of consolidated revenues **and** loss of the customer would have a material adverse effect on the registrant. This is cited as Regulation S-K Item 101(c)(1)(vii) — [SEC staff correspondence citing Item 101(c)(1)(vii)](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001130385/000114420414003473/filename1.htm) (surfaced via search; not crawled)
- Affiliation aggregation: customers under common control or affiliated with one another are treated as a single customer for the 10% test, and the federal government counts as one customer — [Deloitte DART §5.7](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc280-10/roadmap-segment-reporting/chapter-5-entity-wide-disclosures/5-7-information-about-major-customers)
- Deloitte directs SEC registrants to consider "the similar requirements in SEC Regulation S-K, Item 101(c)" alongside ASC 280 — i.e. the accounting standard and the disclosure rule are deliberately different instruments — [Deloitte DART §5.7](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc280-10/roadmap-segment-reporting/chapter-5-entity-wide-disclosures/5-7-information-about-major-customers)
- The 2020 modernisation of Item 101 is real and relevant: the SEC adopted amendments to Items 101, 103 and 105 on 26 Aug 2020 (Release 33-10825; 85 FR 63726, 8 Oct 2020), making Item 101(a) "largely principles-based" and dropping prescribed timeframes — [Federal Register, 85 FR, 8 Oct 2020 (2020-19182)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2020-10-08/pdf/2020-19182.pdf); [Hunton Andrews Kurth, Regulation S-K Modernization, Sep 2020](https://www.hunton.com/media/legal/69475_regulation-s-k-modernization.pdf)
- The **current** codified text does not surface the 10% naming test. A fetch of the consolidated 17 CFR 229.101 returned, for smaller reporting companies, paragraph (c)(4)(vi) "Dependence on one or a few major customers;" and, for other registrants, (c)(1)(i) referring to "any dependence on revenue-generating activities, key products, services, product families or customers" — with no 10% threshold and no naming language — [Cornell LII, 17 CFR 229.101](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/229.101)

### Cited Findings — extraction work and its numbers
- **Latent-edge extraction from 10-Ks [EXT, audit-style].** An LLM pipeline extracts a weighted, directed, point-in-time firm network from 10-K filings of 42 Nasdaq-100 constituents, producing **149 directed edges**; an audit confirmed **88%** of sampled edges with weight ≥0.1, rising to **100%** when "economically plausible but weakly documented" relationships are counted. Refuted edges fell only in the lowest-weight part of the network, and within-sector connectivity rose **1.9×**. The authors report multi-agent fusion, IDF filtering and relative thresholding each improved network quality. Note this is an **audit confirmation rate on a sample, not a precision score against a gold set, and no recall is reported** — [arXiv:2607.15640, 17 Jul 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15640)
- **Snippet-driven supply-chain discovery [EXT; explicit refusal to report precision].** A 2026 pipeline (Serper search snippets → Qwen3-Next-80B → JSON relations, with source-credibility tiers 1–5, Wikidata/Orbis alias matching and Jaro–Winkler similarity) **reports no precision or recall for relationship discovery at all**, and states its credibility tiers are "not interpreted as a precision estimate". Cost comparison on a 100-firm sample, snippets vs full text: 1,362 vs 14,066 HTTP/API requests (10.33×); 3,488 vs 69,024 unique relations (19.79×); 1,945,550 vs 488,646,427 input tokens (251.16×); 558 vs 7,079 input tokens per unique relation; duplicate rate 39.2% vs 71.1% — [Snippet-Driven Supply Chain Discovery with LLMs, arXiv:2605.27845, 27 May 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27845)
- The same paper is unusually candid about completeness: the output is a "web-visible evidence graph, not a complete transaction ledger"; a missing edge "does not establish that no relationship exists" since confidential relationships go unpublished; snippets "sacrifice per-firm recall" versus full text; query-biased summaries may favour publicly prominent relationships, **which the authors have not quantified**; and its listed-firm coverage advantage over CSMAR (7.2× firms, 9.3× relationships) "does not measure recall" — [arXiv:2605.27845](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27845)
- **Earnings-call extraction [EXT].** A 2023 carbon-accounting paper used GPT-3 `davinci-003` to extract buyers, suppliers and items from earnings-call transcripts, reporting a perfect score on precision/recall/F1/accuracy for buyer detection, and recall ≈0.958 with F1 ≈0.979 for suppliers and items. Transcripts, not filings; evaluation-set size and construction need checking before quoting — [Leveraging AI-derived Data for Carbon Accounting, arXiv:2312.03722](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.03722)
- **Benchmark that exists for 10-K relation extraction [EXT].** KPI-EDGAR is a manually annotated set of **81 10-K reports, 1,355 sentences, 4,522 entities, 3,841 relations** — but the relations are KPI-to-value, not supplier-customer — [KPI-EDGAR, arXiv:2210.09163](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.09163)
- Two further 2025–26 papers in this area exist but I could not extract their numbers: a RAG supply-chain mapping paper over 10-Ks and earnings calls validated against S&P Capital IQ as ground truth (paywalled, HTTP 403) — [Supply chain mapping through retrieval-augmented generation, *Journal of the Operational Research Society*, 2025, doi 10.1080/01605682.2025.2608868](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01605682.2025.2608868); and a knowledge-graph + LLM supply-chain visibility paper reporting precision/recall/F1 as mean ± sd over ten runs — [Enhancing supply chain visibility with knowledge graphs and large language models, *IJPR*, 2025, doi 10.1080/00207543.2025.2575841](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00207543.2025.2575841)

### Cited Findings — commercial datasets and licensing
- **FactSet Revere Supply Chain Relationships**: "exposes and classifies the business relationships and interconnections among global companies"; institutional licence, available via WRDS as the Relationships subset. Harvard's own listing shows a licence running 2022 to 2023 expiry, i.e. access is institution-dependent and lapses — [HBS Library](https://www.library.hbs.edu/find/databases/factset-revere-supply-chain-relationships); [Penn Libraries](https://faq.library.upenn.edu/business/faq/45376)
- **Bloomberg SPLC**: the terminal function for a company's suppliers, customers and peers; its supply-chain module tracks customer, supplier and competitive relationships. Terminal-licensed — [Penn Libraries](https://faq.library.upenn.edu/business/faq/45376)
- **Licensing is genuinely contested territory.** Ashbury Heights Capital sued FactSet claiming a 2012 contract (predating FactSet's 2013 acquisition of Revere) required royalties on all FactSet Supply Chain data sales; a 2018 state court sent the claims to trial, and a California state-court jury later returned a near-unanimous defence verdict for FactSet — [WatersTechnology, FactSet buys Revere](https://waterstechnology.com/node/2293059); [CB Insights, Revere Data](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/revere-data)
- **Semi-open option, no accuracy claims.** Equibles sells a supply-chain dataset extracted from 10-K reports — customers, suppliers, competitors, partners, with the quoting sentence attached — and publishes **no accuracy metrics** — [Equibles Supply Chain API](https://equibles.com/supply-chain-api)
- A FactSet-derived supply-chain feed is listed on AWS Marketplace as free, drawing on annual filings, investor presentations and press releases — [AWS Marketplace listing](https://aws.amazon.com/marketplace/pp/prodview-h6mqbgeckx2gk)

### Inferences
- This is still the strongest candidate the project has found for a genuinely undeclared, retrieval-relevant relation in finance — but the literature does not yet support it with the kind of number the series reports. The best figure available is an **88% audit confirmation rate on 149 edges across 42 firms** with no recall at all. That is not a precision figure in the sense the series uses, and it would be dishonest to present it as one.
- The direction of the published evidence is that **precision is achievable and recall is unmeasured**. Every paper that discusses completeness declines to claim it. For a stage-2 step whose correctness criterion *is* completeness, that is the worst possible shape of evidence.
- Licensing resolves the shipping question against a graph-backed finance demo: FactSet Revere and Bloomberg SPLC are institution/terminal-licensed and have been litigated over redistribution, so a public blog series cannot ship either. Equibles or a self-built extraction over a pre-packaged filings corpus are the only publishable routes, and neither comes with a precision number.
- The Reg S-K vs ASC 280 split is a usable narrative beat in its own right: the accounting standard requires the *fact* of concentration and forbids nothing about anonymity, while the disclosure rule's naming obligation is conditional on a materiality judgement made by the issuer. The ground truth for "all companies with exposure to Z" is therefore partly a function of issuer discretion — the relation is not merely undeclared, it is *selectively* declared.

### Gaps
- **The exact current Regulation S-K citation and threshold could not be verified from primary rule text.** Comment letters cite the paragraph variously as (c)(1)(vii) and (c)(1)(viii); the consolidated LII text of 17 CFR 229.101 shows no 10% naming test at all. Either the 2020 modernisation removed/absorbed it into principles-based (c)(1)(i), or LII's rendering is incomplete. I found **no source stating the naming requirement was eliminated**, and the constraint against fetching sec.gov blocked the authoritative check. To settle it: amended Item 101(c) in [85 FR 63726, 8 Oct 2020](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2020-10-08/pdf/2020-19182.pdf) (govinfo, not sec.gov) — do not assert "Item 101(c)(1)(vii), 10%, name required" as current law until that is read.
- No precision/recall figures retrieved for the two 2025 tandfonline supply-chain papers (403 paywall and search-snippet-only). URLs recorded above.
- No published head-to-head of LLM-extracted supply-chain graphs against FactSet Revere or Bloomberg SPLC as ground truth, with precision *and* recall both reported. The RAG mapping paper validates against S&P Capital IQ but its numbers are behind the paywall.

## Q3. Stage 2 as a retrieval problem: set-valued completeness

### Takeaway
Set-valued, exhaustive-answer retrieval is a named, benchmarked task (QUEST, QAMPARI, ListQA, Total Recall QA) and current systems are measurably bad at it — including, specifically, agentic deep-research systems, whose cumulative retrieval recall on entity-set questions is in the low single digits on Wikipedia-derived sets. This is the hardest empirical finding in the whole brief and it lands squarely on stage 2.

### Cited Findings
- **QUEST [RET].** A retrieval dataset of entity-seeking queries with implicit set operations (intersection, union, difference): **3,357 natural-language queries**, each mapped to an exhaustive answer set of 2–20 Wikipedia entities, average ~10.5 answers per query; corpus of 325,505 entity documents; splits 1,307 train / 323 val / 1,727 test — [QUEST, arXiv:2305.11694 (2023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11694) (figures as reported by papers reusing it; see Gaps)
- **QAMPARI** poses questions with at least 5 answers, averaging **13 answers** per question — a deliberate stress test of exhaustive recall rather than top-1 accuracy — as described in [Total Recall QA, arXiv:2603.18516v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18516v2)
- **Total Recall QA (SIGIR '26, v2 dated 11 Sep 2026)** is built precisely on the premise that "accurate generation of the answer requires retrieving **all** relevant documents for a given question", scoring retrieval as recall over relevant entities and answers by Exact Match and Soft Match at 10/20/50/90% tolerances — [arXiv:2603.18516v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18516v2)
- **Its numbers are brutal.** Best single-step **Recall@1000 [RET]**: 84.45% (BGE, Wiki1), 47.13% (E5, Wiki2), 72.75% (SPLADE++, Ecommerce) — with Recall@3 and Recall@10 "much lower". Agentic **cumulative intermediate recall [RET]**: ReAct 2.69 / 2.63 / 13.10; Search-o1 2.49 / 2.28 / 7.07; Search-R1 3.27 / 3.37 / 13.95. Best end-to-end **EM [ANS]**: 11.24 (Wiki1), 6.12 (Wiki2), 2.55 (Ecommerce); the paper says agents "consistently show poor performance across datasets" — [arXiv:2603.18516v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18516v2)
- **Even oracle retrieval does not rescue it [ANS].** With oracle retrieval, GPT-5.2 EM is 56.80 / 27.11 / 5.56 against closed-book 30.18 / 10.33 / 1.67, and the paper reports "a large gap for improvement" remains. Under oracle retrieval, **90.9% (GPT-5.2) to 95.9% (Claude Sonnet 4.5) of errors were reasoning errors**, not parametric-knowledge bias — [arXiv:2603.18516v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18516v2)
- **The failure is a query-planning failure, not a ranking failure.** Agents' sub-query counts stay roughly constant regardless of how many gold entities a query has; as they issue more sub-queries they mainly retrieve distracting entities. The authors conclude deep-research agents "cannot effectively issue queries for finding all relevant documents" — [arXiv:2603.18516v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18516v2)
- **ListQA (2026)** scores LLM-generated lists on completeness and on avoidance of fabricated items — i.e. the field has converged on *completeness plus non-fabrication* as the right pair of axes for set answers — [ListQA, arXiv:2610.19165](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.19165)
- Retrieval architectures specifically for set/Boolean semantics exist and are an open problem: SetBERT targets Boolean-logic and set-operation queries — [SetBERT, arXiv:2406.17282](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.17282) — and QO-Bench diagnoses whether retrieval preserves query operators over typed tuples — [QO-Bench, arXiv:2606.04646v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04646v2)

### Inferences
- Hit@4 is the wrong metric for stage 2 and the series should say so explicitly. Hit@4 asks whether one correct passage appeared; stage 2's correctness criterion is "no member of the set was missed". Those are different questions, and the oracle-retrieval numbers above show that even perfect retrieval leaves most of the task unsolved.
- Oracle-retrieval EM of 56.80 at best sets a ceiling: if the series fed stage 2 a hand-curated perfect exposure set, the published evidence says a strong model would still fail the aggregation on nearly half of comparable questions. That is a useful, honest framing for "this question shape is not pipeline-ready", and it holds *independently* of whether a graph improves stage 2.
- There is a real argument *for* a graph here that earlier project research did not have to face: the set-completeness failure in TRQA is a query-planning failure, and a materialised edge list turns "issue the right sub-queries" into "traverse". That does not make GraphRAG's *global search* the answer (see Q4) — it makes a plain relational edge table the answer, once the edges exist. The hard part stays extraction (Q2), not the data structure.

### Gaps
- QUEST's headline figures above come from papers reusing it and from a dataset aggregator rather than from a direct read of the QUEST paper. Verify against [arXiv:2305.11694](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.11694) before quoting the 3,357 / 2–20 / 325,505 numbers.
- No set-completeness benchmark exists over financial filings. All of the above is Wikipedia/e-commerce. The series would be the first to measure it on a finance corpus, which is an opportunity and a caveat.

## Q4. Stage 4: synthesis, and whether "consensus" is well defined

### Takeaway
Yes — this question shape is the regime GraphRAG was built and evaluated for, and saying so is the honest reading of its paper; but its evaluation instrument (LLM-judge pairwise preference on comprehensiveness and diversity, no gold answers) cannot tell you whether the consensus is *right*, only whether it reads as broader. Separately, "consensus" is a load-bearing term of art in finance: it denotes an average of *analyst numeric estimates* assembled by I/B/E/S, Zacks and similar, and applying it to a synthesis of company prose would be importing a precision the pipeline does not deliver.

### Cited Findings — GraphRAG's regime
- GraphRAG's own framing: corpus-wide questions such as "What are the main themes in the dataset?" are "inherently a query-focused summarization (QFS) task, rather than an explicit retrieval task", and existing QFS methods do not scale to RAG-sized corpora — [From Local to Global: A GraphRAG Approach to Query-Focused Summarization, arXiv:2404.16130v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v2)
- Method: LLM extracts entities, relationships and claims per chunk into a weighted entity graph; Leiden community detection builds a hierarchy; community summaries are pre-generated; answers come from a **map-reduce pass over those summaries** — [arXiv:2404.16130v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v2)
- Evaluation [ANS]: two ~1M-token corpora (podcast transcripts and news/MultiHop-RAG); LLM-generated questions with N = 5 giving **125 test questions per dataset**; LLM-as-judge head-to-head against vector RAG and a text-summarisation baseline; headline claim is gains in **comprehensiveness and diversity** — [arXiv:2404.16130v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v2)
- The one coverage-style check that is not a judge preference: global conditions extracted **31–34 average claims per response versus 25–26 for vector RAG** — more claims, not more correct claims — [arXiv:2404.16130v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v2); corroborated by [secondary review of the paper](https://themoonlight.io/es/review/from-local-to-global-a-graph-rag-approach-to-query-focused-summarization)
- The regime boundary is stated in the secondary literature as well: global GraphRAG conditions consistently outperform semantic search on sensemaking questions, while **semantic search performs better on specific lookup queries** — [secondary review](https://themoonlight.io/es/review/from-local-to-global-a-graph-rag-approach-to-query-focused-summarization)

### Cited Findings — architectures for "one document per entity, then aggregate"
- **Hierarchical / multi-stage summarisation [ANS].** NEXUSSUM (ACL 2025) is a hierarchical multi-LLM pipeline reporting gains "up to 30%" over baselines, largest on BookSum, attributed to reduced context truncation; it beats HM-SR by 7.1% on MovieSum, credited to structured length control — [NEXUSSUM, ACL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.500.pdf)
- **Context-aware hierarchical merging [ANS, claim-level].** With Llama-3.1-70B on book summarisation, an Extract-Support variant had the highest share of **correct claims**, ~10 points above baselines, while an Extract-Replace variant fell more than 10 points *below* baseline — i.e. merging strategy choice swings claim correctness by ~20 points in either direction — [arXiv:2502.00977](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.00977)
- **Tree-structured map-reduce [ANS].** ToM (EMNLP 2025) reports beating divide-and-conquer and RAG baselines on 70B+ models, with ablations showing the **reduce phase matters most** — [ToM, EMNLP 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.899.pdf)
- **Per-subset agents then an editor.** PerSphere/HierSphere has local agents summarise subsets of retrieved documents and an editorial agent merge them, motivated by models struggling with long contexts — [PerSphere, arXiv:2412.12588](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.12588)
- **Retrieve-then-extract-then-aggregate, as deep-research products do it, is now benchmarked [ANS].** DeepResearch Bench pairs a reference-based judge (RACE) with a citation framework (FACT) measuring effective citation count and citation accuracy; Perplexity Deep Research led on citation accuracy, Gemini-2.5-Pro Deep Research on comprehensiveness and effective citation count — [DeepResearch Bench, arXiv:2506.11763](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11763)
- DRACO grades factual accuracy, breadth/depth, presentation and citation quality across 100 tasks in 10 domains — [DRACO, arXiv:2602.11685v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11685v1). DEER uses 101 rubric items plus information verification and concludes current systems produce "structurally plausible, evidence-citing reports" that still fail to achieve "logical completeness" — [DEER, arXiv:2512.17776v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.17776v1). ReportBench finds "substantial room for improvement in terms of the breadth and depth of research coverage, as well as factual consistency" — [ReportBench, arXiv:2508.15804](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15804)

### Cited Findings — what "consensus" actually means in finance
- I/B/E/S summary consensus is built from analyst estimates on a specific accounting basis: only estimates "prepared on the accounting basis adopted by the majority of contributing analysts for a given forecast period" enter the calculation; minority-basis estimates are excluded and footnoted. The basis is typically settled ~5 business days after reported earnings and ~3 business days after guidance announcements — [WRDS, I/B/E/S Additional Expert Questions](https://wrds-www.wharton.upenn.edu/documents/2251/IBES_Additional_Expert_Questions_Clean_Final.pdf)
- Scale of the panel: I/B/E/S holds forecasts from more than 30,000 analysts at more than 3,000 brokerage firms — [Aalto data-source description of I/B/E/S](https://datahub.aalto.fi/en/data-sources/ibes-estimates-ibes)
- The mean/median are explicitly described in the literature as point estimates of the analyst forecast *distribution*, and that distribution is non-normal — one study of Australian I/B/E/S data argues a better consensus measure than the plain mean or median may exist — [Leung (2011), *International Journal of Banking and Finance*](https://e-journal.uum.edu.my/index.php/ijbf/article/view/8428)
- Zacks runs *several different* consensus constructions, which is itself the point: the "most accurate" consensus is "the average of the top quartile of the analysts making forecasts, for whom we have history", ranked by past absolute forecast error versus the consensus error; the Average Brokerage Recommendation is a separate 1–5 ratings measure ("1 represents a Strong Buy and 5 represents a Strong Sell") — [Zacks Pro, Current Ratings and EPS Estimates](https://www.zackspro.com/tools/help/helpcasc/c_currentest.htm); [Zacks Pro, Average Recommendations Report](https://www.zackspro.com/da_3.asp)

### Inferences
- **State it plainly in the series:** the COMPOSITE question is a query-focused multi-document summarisation question, and that is the one regime where GraphRAG's own paper claims and measures a win. This does not contradict the project's earlier factoid finding — it confirms the boundary the GraphRAG authors themselves draw, with factoid lookup on the other side of it.
- But the comparison must carry the instrument with it. GraphRAG's win is 125 LLM-generated questions per corpus, judged pairwise on comprehensiveness and diversity with no gold answers; its only non-preference evidence is *more claims per response*. A series whose currency is TREC qrels, Hit@4 and a confidently-wrong rate would be swapping a falsifiable metric for an unfalsifiable one at exactly the stage where it matters most. "More comprehensive" and "more claims" are both compatible with "more confidently wrong".
- **"Consensus" is the wrong word and should be changed.** In finance it names a specific structured product: a filtered average of numeric analyst estimates, with documented rules about accounting basis, timing and (at Zacks) analyst selection. The pipeline would be producing a prose synthesis of company self-descriptions — a different object, from different authors, with no estimate distribution behind it. Honest alternative wordings: *"a synthesised summary of what each exposed company says about its own outlook"*, *"an aggregated view of self-reported outlooks"*, or *"a cross-company digest of outlook language"*. Each keeps the demo and drops the false claim. Reserving the word "consensus" for the analyst-estimate sense also leaves the series room to contrast the two, which is a better blog post than conflating them.
- Architecturally, the published trade-off is: map-reduce is parallel and simple but loses cross-chunk facts; hierarchical reduction wins on very long inputs where a single reduce step drops information; iterative refinement preserves narrative continuity at the cost of serialisation. The claim-correctness swing in arXiv:2502.00977 (~+10 vs ~−10 points on the same task) says the merge strategy is not a detail — it is the dominant design choice in stage 4.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head benchmark comparing map-reduce, hierarchical merging and iterative refinement under a single protocol was found; the comparison has to be assembled across papers with different datasets and metrics.
- No official I/B/E/S or Zacks methodology document stating how the *headline* consensus mean is weighted, or how stale estimates are aged out, was located. The WRDS and Zacks Pro pages above are the best available primary-ish sources.
- Vendor and practitioner numbers encountered for map-reduce vs refine ("28% faster", "68% failure rate for naive chunking") had no traceable primary source and are deliberately excluded.

## Q5. Error compounding across the three stages, and attribution of a synthesised answer

### Takeaway
There is no standard published formula for error propagation specific to RAG; the multiplicative p^n model is the common starting point and is an upper bound because stages are not independent. What *is* well documented, and directly usable, is that models over-answer at 65–91% rates under imperfect evidence, that output confidence is a near-useless signal for whether an answer was admissible (0.54–0.67 AUROC), and that citation faithfulness on long-form answers holds roughly half the time — so a confident-sounding synthesis over an incomplete set is the expected behaviour, not an edge case.

### Cited Findings — compounding
- The multiplicative model: for n sequential steps each succeeding with probability p, end-to-end success is p^n; worked through, ten steps at 95%/90%/85% per-step give roughly **59% / 35% / 20%** end-to-end. Real pipelines are not independent, so treat as a rough bound — [Zartis, The Compounding Errors Problem](https://www.zartis.com/the-compounding-errors-problem-why-multi-agent-systems-fail-and-the-architecture-that-fixes-it/) (practitioner source; the arithmetic is self-evident, the applicability is the claim)
- **Stage attribution is itself a research problem.** RAGEC classifies RAG errors by the stage that caused them (chunking, retrieval, reranking, generation), attributing to the *first* stage where an error appears, and runs its checks **backward** from generation to chunking because a forward pass misclassified chunking errors without downstream context — [Classifying and Addressing the Diversity of Errors in Retrieval-Augmented Generation Systems, arXiv:2510.13975v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13975v1)
- **Ablation as the practical measurement [ANS].** IteraSim measures each stage's contribution by removing it: disabling multi-query expansion dropped its composite score from **75.6% to 25.9%** — a single stage carrying ~50 points of end-to-end performance — [IteraSim RAG, arXiv:2607.20346](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.20346)
- **Aggregate scores hide stage regressions.** A single end-to-end score can mask retrieval regressions because generation-side metrics such as faithfulness stay flat while context quality falls; the recommendation is separate retrieval and generation scorecards with Recall@k, Precision@k, MRR and nDCG tracked over time — [TianPan, Measuring the Wrong Half of Your RAG Pipeline, 5 Jul 2026](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/07/05/measuring-the-wrong-half-of-your-rag-pipeline) (practitioner source)
- Cascading hallucination in agentic RAG is now being framed as its own detection problem — [CHARM, arXiv:2606.04435](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.04435)

### Cited Findings — does synthesis increase confident wrongness
- **Over-answering is the norm, not the exception.** Under conflicting evidence, all evaluated models show **over-answer rates of 65% to 91%**; chain-of-thought improves abstention for some models but inconsistently across families — [Evaluating RAG Reliability under Clean, Misleading, and Mixed Retrieval, arXiv:2606.07783](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.07783)
- Given irrelevant retrieved text, models hallucinate false answers **over 50% of the time** zero-shot, and few-shot in-context learning is "ineffective at mitigating" it — [Retrieval Augmented Question Answering: When Should LLMs Admit Ignorance?, arXiv:2512.23836](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23836)
- **Self-reported confidence cannot detect the failure.** Trained readouts from output confidence reach only **0.54–0.67 AUROC** on whether an answer attempt was admissible, while hidden-state readouts reach **0.97–0.99** on the same task; ordinary answer-confidence mostly signals whether an attempted answer succeeded, not whether attempting was legitimate — [Two Axes of LLM Abstention, arXiv:2607.08456v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08456v1)
- Prompt-based abstention specifically fails under misleading context in small frozen RAG models — [arXiv:2608.22228](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22228)
- Distinguishing *missing* evidence from *incompletely retrieved* evidence is a measurable, separate win: a logical-search agentic method raised refusal on answer-unavailable questions from **0.767 to 0.828** and lowered hallucination from **0.128 to 0.083** — [Rethinking Agentic RAG, arXiv:2605.27123](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.27123)
- Grounding/abstention under contextual evidence is being approached with RL — [GRACE, arXiv:2601.04525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04525)

### Cited Findings — attribution of a synthesised answer
- **ALCE** evaluates long-form answers with citations using an NLI model: **citation recall** (is each statement entailed by its cited passages) and **citation precision** (is each citation necessary) — [ALCE (Gao et al.), OpenReview](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=bxFwIn0wZ0); [summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2305.14627)
- **The compression/attribution trade-off is the exact stage-4 problem.** Summarising or selecting snippets raises answer coverage but weakens citation fidelity through information loss; on ELI5 the best models supported their citations in only **about half** of generations — [ALCE](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=bxFwIn0wZ0)
- Post-hoc versus inline citation differ sharply [ANS]: on ALCE, post-hoc citation reached **75% coverage with 42% correctness** versus **37% coverage and 21% correctness** for generation-time citation — [Generation-Time vs. Post-hoc Citation, arXiv:2509.21557](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21557)
- An answer can be *correct* while its citations fail to support its claims, and exact-match scoring cannot handle long-form answers at all — [Do LLM Attribution Metrics Transfer?, arXiv:2606.23915](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23915)
- NLI-based support scores do not establish that the generator actually *relied* on the cited evidence — so passing a citation check is not evidence of grounded reasoning — [The Attribution-Compression Frontier in RAG, arXiv:2609.14245](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14245)
- Active lines on faithful inline citation for long-form QA — [CAGE, arXiv:2607.24236](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24236); [FineRef, arXiv:2602.18437](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18437); [CiteCheck, arXiv:2502.10881](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.10881)

### Inferences
- **This is the most important finding for the series.** The three-stage COMPOSITE pipeline puts an over-answering synthesiser downstream of a step (set resolution) that the literature says current systems fail at. The 65–91% over-answer rate and the 0.54–0.67 AUROC on confidence mean the pipeline's most likely failure mode is a fluent, well-cited-looking consensus over a set that is silently missing members — exactly the thing the series' "confidently wrong" metric exists to catch, and a case where the metric's underlying cause is invisible to the metric.
- Synthesis plausibly *raises* measured confident wrongness for a structural reason the sources support indirectly: GraphRAG's global mode produces **31–34 claims per response versus 25–26** for vector RAG, and claim-level correctness on hierarchical merging swings by ~20 points with merge strategy. More claims per answer at unchanged per-claim reliability means more wrong claims per answer. No paper measures this directly as "synthesis increases confident wrongness" — the series could be the one that does, by reporting its confidently-wrong rate per claim *and* per answer, before and after adding a synthesis stage. That is a cheap, novel, honest experiment.
- Can a consensus statement be cited to source passages? Partially, and badly. The ALCE line gives the instrument (NLI-based citation precision/recall), and the honest summary of its results is ~50% citation support on long-form answers, 42% correctness at best for post-hoc citation. For a consensus claim — which is by construction a statement about the *aggregate*, entailed by no single passage — the NLI formulation is additionally ill-posed: no retrieved passage entails "most exposed companies are cautious". **Attribution of genuinely aggregative claims is effectively unevaluated**; the benchmarks evaluate attribution of extractive and abstractive-but-local claims.
- Practically: the series should keep the three metric families separate per stage — Recall over the gold exposure set [RET] for stage 2, per-entity label/span accuracy [EXT] for stage 3, and judge/citation metrics [ANS] for stage 4 — and resist any single composite score. The TianPan and RAGEC points both land here, and the backward-attribution finding says debug from the answer back to the chunk, not forward.

### Gaps
- **No published measurement of compounding error for a set-resolution → per-entity-extraction → synthesis pipeline specifically.** The closest artefacts are stage-ablation studies (IteraSim), stage-attribution taxonomies (RAGEC) and the agentic recall collapse in Total Recall QA. The multiplicative bound is folklore-grade, sourced only to a practitioner post.
- **No work found that measures whether a synthesis stage increases confident wrongness relative to the same evidence presented un-synthesised.** This is a clear negative: the question is open, and it is the single most publishable gap the series is positioned to close.
- No evaluation found of attribution for *aggregative* claims (claims true of a set, entailed by no individual passage). Flag as unevaluated rather than hard.

## Q6. Outlook extraction, and the time dimension in "current outlook"

### Takeaway
Forward-looking-statement detection and financial sentiment are well-benchmarked as *sentence classification* with F1/accuracy, but "outlook" as the series means it — a directional judgement about a company's prospects — is not a benchmarked task, and the gap between the two is where this stage will fail. On time: "current outlook" has three distinct readings, and only the *as-known-at-a-date* reading uses the series' bitemporal machinery; the other two throw it away.

### Cited Findings — datasets, benchmarks and metrics
- **FLS classification [EXT].** A forward-looking-statement dataset is used as one of nine benchmarks in an LLM financial-NLP evaluation; it classifies whether a sentence contains a forward-looking statement and contains **3,500 manually annotated sentences from the MD&A sections of annual reports of Russell 3000 firms** — [Is ChatGPT a Financial Expert? Evaluating Language Models on Financial Natural Language Processing, arXiv:2310.12664 (2023)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12664) (dataset description from search extraction; see Gaps)
- **FinBen [EXT/ANS].** 36 datasets, 24 financial tasks across seven aspects (information extraction, textual analysis, QA, text generation, risk management, forecasting, decision-making); first to include stock trading, agent and RAG evaluation; 15 LLMs evaluated including GPT-4, ChatGPT and Gemini; headline conclusion is that LLMs do well on information extraction and textual analysis but struggle with complex reasoning, text generation and **forecasting** — [FinBen, arXiv:2402.12659, submitted 20 Feb 2024 (v2 19 Jun 2024)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12659); NeurIPS 2024 D&B track version — [proceedings PDF](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/adb1d9fa8be4576d28703b396b82ba1b-Paper-Datasets_and_Benchmarks_Track.pdf)
- **Metrics, verified from the leaderboard card [EXT].** Financial PhraseBank (FPB) sentiment classification: "Accuracy" and "F1-Score". FiQA-SA: "F1-Score" only. FOMC hawkish-dovish classification: "F1-Score" and "Accuracy". The Open FinLLM Leaderboard lists **40 tasks and contains no FLS task** — [Open FinLLM Leaderboard, Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/blog/leaderboard-finbench)
- BloombergGPT is the source that pins down weighting for FPB: 5-shot, "F1 score weighted by support", on a test split of **970 sentences** — [BloombergGPT, arXiv:2303.17564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.17564)
- **FinSen** is a market-news dataset: economic and financial news from 197 countries with stock-market data, 2007–2023, **160,000 records** — but its Kaggle mirror states sentiment and scores are **generated by the FinBERT model**, i.e. the labels are model-generated, not human-annotated; the authors' own use is forecasting and calibration (Focal Calibration Loss) — [FinSen on Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/eaglewhl/finsen-financial-sentiment-dataset)
- FinBERT itself is the standard pretrained baseline for financial sentiment — [FinBERT, arXiv:1908.10063](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10063)
- FLaME is a 2025 finance LM evaluation framework covering these task families — [FLaME, arXiv:2506.15846](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15846)

### Cited Findings — safe harbour and defensive drafting
- Courts have repeatedly held that generic forward-looking disclaimers do **not** earn the PSLRA safe harbour. The D.C. Circuit in *Harman* treated generic disclaimers as insufficient, reasoning that such language is general and ubiquitous and so does not help investors — [Mintz, D.C. Circuit Holds "Boilerplate" Forward-Looking Statements Are Not Entitled to PSLRA's Safe Harbor (Aug 2015)](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2451/2015-08-dc-circuit-holds-boilerplate-forward-looking-statements-are); [Dykema, Is Your Forward-Looking Statement Safe Harbor Safe?](https://www.dykema.com/news-insights/is-your-forward-looking-statement-safe-harbor-safe.html)
- The Seventh Circuit's *Asher* takes a more balanced line, requiring warnings of risks "of similar significance" to what actually occurred — [USLawExplained, Forward-Looking Statements](https://uslawexplained.com/forward-looking_statement)
- Counsel advise reusable templates **tailored per use** rather than identical paragraphs repeated across filings, and warn against attaching the legend to documents containing no forward-looking statements — [Venable, Forward-Looking Statements: Safe Harbors Compliance Guidelines, Sep 2024](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/09/forward-looking-statements-safe-harbors-comp); [Dykema](https://www.dykema.com/news-insights/is-your-forward-looking-statement-safe-harbor-safe.html)
- Guidance does appear in extractable, numeric form in calls and releases: e.g. a raised full-year revenue outlook above $3 billion in a Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript — [Benchmark Electronics Q3 2026 transcript, 29 Oct 2026](https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/29/benchmark-electronics-bhe-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/)
- Companies publish the trigger-word list themselves: decks enumerate the cue words used to flag forward-looking statements ("expects", "anticipates", "outlook"), which is a ready-made labelling scheme — [Evergy Q2 2026 earnings call deck, 6 Aug 2026](https://investors.evergy.com/static-files/73352465-a5e0-4dbf-ac4b-98692902443f)

### Inferences
- The benchmarks measure the wrong thing for stage 3, and it should be said plainly. FLS classification asks *is this sentence forward-looking*; FPB/FiQA/FOMC ask *what polarity is this sentence*. The COMPOSITE pipeline needs *what is this company's directional outlook, overall, right now* — a document-level judgement with no benchmark, no gold labels and no reported metric anywhere I found. Stage 3 would be unmeasurable with off-the-shelf instruments, which is worse than it being hard.
- Safe-harbour boilerplate does **not** defeat outlook extraction, but it changes the target. The legal pressure runs *against* boilerplate (generic disclaimers lose the safe harbour), so the substantive, tailored risk language is legally advantaged and therefore present. The practical consequence is a precision/recall nuisance — high-volume, near-duplicate legend text that any cue-word extractor will fire on — not an information-theoretic wall. The cheap mitigation is a boilerplate filter before extraction, and the series can measure its effect directly.
- The deeper problem is that filings are drafted defensively and symmetrically: a careful MD&A states both upside and downside for the same driver. A polarity classifier over such text will produce a near-neutral aggregate for almost every company, which then makes the stage-4 "consensus" vacuous. Guidance in earnings-call remarks and releases (numeric, directional, revised) is a far better stage-3 target than MD&A prose, and the Benchmark Electronics example shows the shape of it.
- **On time, there are three readings and only one keeps the bitemporal machinery:** (a) *as of the latest filing* — a single as-of-now snapshot, which needs only a current index and discards bitemporality; (b) *as of a chosen date* — valid-time only, one axis, a point-in-time corpus; (c) *as known at a date* — both axes, valid time plus the time the statement was filed/available, which is exactly bitemporal and the only reading that makes restatements, late filings and amended guidance answerable. Reading (c) is also the only one that makes the question *scientifically* answerable, because it fixes the information set and so makes a wrong answer identifiable as wrong.
- Independent support for (c) being the live research framing: the latent-edge paper's contribution is explicitly a **point-in-time** economic graph built from disclosures, i.e. the one serious attempt at this relation type treats as-of-date construction as a first-class requirement rather than an afterthought — [arXiv:2607.15640](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15640)

### Gaps
- Could not verify the FLS dataset's evaluation metric or reported scores from the paper text (PDF text streams unreadable to the fetch tool; HF dataset card `TheFinAI/en-fls` returned HTTP 401). The 3,500-sentence / Russell 3000 MD&A description comes from search extraction of [arXiv:2310.12664](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12664) and should be confirmed before quoting. Could not confirm whether FinBen includes an FLS task at all — its abstract does not enumerate tasks, and the Open FinLLM Leaderboard's 40 tasks do not include one.
- **No benchmark found for document-level or company-level "outlook" extraction**, and none for forward-looking-statement *extraction* (as opposed to sentence classification) dated 2025–2026. Searches for 2026 forward-looking-statement benchmarks returned only earnings-call transcripts and disclosure decks. Record this as a clear negative: the task the series would need to measure does not have a public benchmark.
- **No empirical accounting/finance literature located on whether boilerplate cautionary language is informative to investors.** The entire boilerplate discussion above is legal-practice commentary on safe-harbour doctrine, not evidence about information content. A targeted search of the risk-factor-disclosure literature would be needed.
- FinSen's record count (160,000) and date range come from the Kaggle mirror rather than the authors' paper; its labels being FinBERT-generated makes it unsuitable as a gold standard regardless.
