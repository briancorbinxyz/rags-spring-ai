Scope note: this covers time as a **filter** over a versioned corpus ("as of T"), not recency-as-a-ranking-feature temporal IR. A short contrastive note is in the final section.

## The core line of work: what exactly is a time-travel inverted index?

### Takeaway
A time-travel inverted index is an ordinary inverted index whose postings carry a **valid-time interval** — `(d, tf, [t_i, t_j))` instead of `(d, tf)` — so that one posting exists per (term, document-version). This is catastrophically redundant (reported ~47x more postings and ~92x more bytes on Wikipedia), and the whole research line is about buying that space back: *temporal coalescing* (merge temporally adjacent postings with near-identical payloads, error-bounded by epsilon) and *sublist materialisation / slicing* (replicate postings into per-time-interval sublists to shorten scans).

### Cited Findings
- Posting format: "each inverted-list posting is a triple of document ID, payload, and a validity time interval `[t_b, t_e]`"; the payload is typically the term's tf, optionally positions — [FluxCapacitor, VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- Exact posting semantics in the long version: "the posting `(d, tf, t_i, t_j)` in the inverted list belonging to term v expresses that for term v, document d had score tf during the time interval `[t_i, t_j)`. We thus obtain one such posting `(d, tf, t_i, t_i+1)` for each term v occurring in a document version `d_ti`." — [Berberich, Bedathur, Weikum, "Efficient Time-Travel on Versioned Text Collections", BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- List sort order in the BTW/I2T2 design: **descending score primary**, ascending docid secondary, increasing left validity-interval boundary tertiary (to support the TC-NRA threshold algorithm) — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- **The space blow-up, stated directly**: a non-temporal index over the full English Wikipedia is ~**184,825,636 postings ≈ 2.8 GB** (at 16 bytes/posting); the temporally extended index over 5 years of revision history is ~**8,634,711,830 postings, > 257 GB** (at 32 bytes/extended posting) — [VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- Temporal coalescing, accurate form: coalesce temporally adjacent postings of the same document **only if their tf payloads are identical**. Approximate form: coalesce if payloads are *similar*, with maximum error bounded by a tunable epsilon; equivalent to computing an error-bounded piecewise-constant (histogram) representation of each document's tf time series, solved by a single-pass linear-time algorithm with a close-to-optimal number of output postings — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content); [VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- In the demo's worked example, coalescing takes a term's posting count for one document from **9 to 3** — [VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- Measured coalescing effect (BTW 2007, 20% sample of English Wikipedia revision history, minor edits excluded: **892,255 documents, 2,795,383 versions, mean 3.13 versions/doc, sd 14.24**). Non-coalesced index: **1,244,168,879 postings**. Then, postings (ratio vs accurate-coalesced baseline):
  - tf-idf: epsilon=0% (accurate) 1,129,472,027 (1.0000); 1% **180,196,069 (0.1595)**; 5% 178,581,509 (0.1581); 10% 177,003,930 (0.1567)
  - Okapi BM25: epsilon=0% 1,186,996,012 (1.0000); 1% 595,180,256 (0.5014); 5% 327,687,360 (0.2761); 10% **245,488,770 (0.2068)**
  — [BTW 2007, Table 1](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- Quality cost of coalescing: overlap and Kendall's tau versus the epsilon=0% baseline "decrease gracefully"; at epsilon=10% with tf-idf, on an index only ~16% of the original size, the top-100 list contains **on average 75 documents** also present in the baseline list. The conclusion states the index can be reduced "close to an order of magnitude, while retaining Kendall's tau value close to **0.8** and about **75% content overlap in the top-100**" — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- Processing cost: as epsilon goes 0% -> 10%, the number of sorted accesses needed for the query batch "reduces by nearly an order of magnitude"; for tf-idf there is a **sharp drop at epsilon=1%** followed by almost no further decrease (mirroring the index-size curve) — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- The top-k operator is **TC-NRA** (Temporal-Context-aware NRA), an extension of the Fagin et al. threshold/NRA family, guaranteed to produce the correct top-k — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- Document-level score aggregation across the versions alive in a query window: MAX, MIN, and TAVG (mean version relevance; "a simple sum of version relevance scores would favor frequently changing documents"). MIN is the most expensive, needing **up to one order of magnitude more sorted accesses** than MAX/TAVG — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- The 2007 system is called **I2T2** ("immortal index"); the demo prototype is **FluxCapacitor**; the 2010 dissertation calls the structure the Time-Travel Inverted Index (TTIX) — [VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf); [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- Experimental platform (BTW 2007): Java JDK 1.5, SUN V40z, 4x AMD Opteron, 16 GB RAM, RAID-5 array, **all data held in an Oracle 10g database**; 45 AOL-log queries x 6 temporal contexts (3 years + 3 random quarters) x indexes x aggregation models = 6,480 processed queries, top-100 — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- Canonical citation for the SIGIR version: Berberich, Bedathur, Neumann, Weikum, "A time machine for text search", SIGIR 2007, DOI 10.1145/1277741.1277831 — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1277741.1277831)
- Prior art the authors credit as the first notable exception: **Anick and Flynn (1992), "Versioning a full-text information retrieval system"** — versions the *entire index* for historical queries; "access costs are optimized for accesses to the most recent versions and increase" for older ones — [BTW 2007 related work](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)

### Inferences
- The two knobs are orthogonal and point in opposite directions. Coalescing is pure lossy compression of the tf time series: it shrinks the index *and* speeds queries, at a bounded ranking cost. Slicing/sublist materialisation *grows* the index to speed queries. The "space-vs-performance knob" in the SIGIR 2007 abstract is really the second one; coalescing is a near-free win.
- The BM25 vs tf-idf asymmetry in Table 1 is the most actionable detail: BM25 payloads are document-length-normalised and therefore change value on *every* edit, so accurate coalescing recovers almost nothing (1.187B of 1.244B postings remain, ~5%) and even epsilon=1% only halves the index. The raw tf payload of tf-idf is far more stable (epsilon=1% gets to 16%). **If you normalise inside the payload, you destroy coalescibility.** Keeping raw tf in the posting and applying length normalisation at query time is what makes the compression work.
- Accurate (lossless) coalescing alone is nearly worthless at these version rates (1.244B -> 1.129B postings, ~9% for tf-idf). The whole win is in the *approximate* regime.

- The SIGIR 2007 paper's own experimental table (as distinct from BTW 2007's) could not be retrieved; the MPI report host was down and no open mirror surfaced. I could find no evidence that the SIGIR version reports Kendall's tau; the tau=0.8 / 75%-overlap figures are from the BTW 2007 companion. Treat any claim that "SIGIR 2007 reported tau" as unverified.
- The formal statements of the sublist-materialisation optimisation problems (named SOPT/POPT, performance-guarantee PG, space-bound SB in MPI lecture slides) could not be read from a primary source: the slide deck at `resources.mpi-inf.mpg.de` returns HTTP 403 and the tech report host is down. ## Index partitioning strategies for versioned corpora

### Takeaway
Two orthogonal cuts exist. **Slicing** partitions each posting list along the *time* axis; entries whose valid-time interval crosses a boundary must be replicated, and the published guidance is that reaching acceptable query efficiency "necessarily" costs a **2x-3x index size increase**. **Sharding** (Anand et al., SIGIR 2011) rotates the cut 90 degrees and partitions each list along *document identifiers*, producing disjoint shards with **~1% space overhead** that still beat the best tuned sliced index on every query granularity tested.

### Cited Findings
- Slicing defined: "time-enriched inverted lists are sliced into several smaller sublists each spanning a specific subinterval of the time axis... every entry whose valid-time interval spans the boundaries of more than one slice is replicated in all the slices which overlap" — [Anand, Bedathur, Berberich, Schenkel, "Temporal Index Sharding for Space-Time Efficiency in Archive Search", MPI-I-2011-5-001 / SIGIR 2011](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- **Published guidance on boundaries and blow-up**: "While a careful choice of the slicing boundaries can help to reduce the index blowup [Berberich et al. 2007] in order to achieve acceptable levels of efficiency, **2 to 3 times index size increase is necessary**." The authors also note that optimal slicing (where no time-travel query reads a superfluous entry) "is impractical to achieve due to the resulting blowup" — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- The space-bound (SB) formulation is confirmed as the tunable: "the parameter gamma denotes the space restriction that models the maximum blowup in index size relative to a non-partitioned index"; the 2011 experiments use gamma = {1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0} (SB-1.5 ... SB-3.0) with the original authors' own latest implementation — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Sharding: partition along docids, "an immediate benefit of this reoriented partitioning is almost **no increase** in the overall index-size... only overhead is to maintain a small set of location pointers in each partition" (the per-shard **impact list**: begin-times as keys, offsets from shard start as values, read fully into RAM at day granularity). Headline: "our sharded temporal index has a negligible ~**1% increase in index size**, but outperforms an optimized implementation of the previous best approach" — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- **"Staircase" is a property of a shard, not a time-partitioning scheme.** A shard has the staircase property when its entries form a temporal-subsumption pattern, so that one seek into the shard followed by a sequential scan reads only required entries. A single-pass greedy algorithm (assign each entry, in begin-time order, to the staircase-respecting shard with minimum gap, else open a new shard; shard ends stay sorted so placement is a binary search) is proved to produce the **optimal** (minimum-cardinality) sharding — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Because random access costs more than sequential read, idealised sharding over-partitions; the paper adds **cost-aware relaxed merging** with a relaxation parameter equal to the storage system's random/sequential I/O cost ratio (Cr/Cs, "usually in the order of 100 and 1000"), giving RS-10 / RS-100 / RS-1000. Crucially, this tuning "is **independent** of the dynamics in the query workload, as it depends only on the storage system parameters" — unlike slicing, which must be tuned to the query workload's typical time-interval length — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Datasets: **WIKI** = English Wikipedia revision history Jan 2001-Dec 2005, **0.7 TB** raw uncompressed, minor edits excluded: **1,517,524 documents, 15,079,829 versions**, mean 9.94 versions/doc, sd 46.08. **UKGOV** = European Archive subset, weekly crawls of **11** UK government sites, text/plain + text/html only, **0.4 TB**: **685,678 documents, 17,297,548 versions**, mean 25.23, sd 28.38. Timestamps rounded to day granularity — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Shard counts: WIKI mean index list length 496,259 entries; idealised sharding (IS) gives **79.75 shards/list**, falling to 32.5 (RS-10), 11.78 (RS-100), 4.71 (RS-1000). Shard size as a fraction of the whole list: 2-8% (IS), 7-20% (RS-10), 35-50% (RS-1000). UKGOV mean list length 945,044; IS 17.86 shards/list -> 14.36 / 9.9 / 4.76; sizes 1-2% (IS) -> 7-8% (RS-10) -> 4-44% (RS-1000) — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Latency, wall-clock ms, warm cache, single core, conjunctive semantics, 7-bit variable-byte encoding, index on disk:
  - WIKI day queries: SB-3.0 **10.63 ms** vs RS-1000 **10.26 ms** (near parity, slicing's best case)
  - WIKI month queries: RS-1000 is **19.5%** faster than SB-3.0
  - WIKI year queries: RS-1000 **22.2%** faster than SB-1.5 (slicing's best year/full-lifetime config); full-lifetime **19%** faster
  - UKGOV day queries: RS-1000 **69.88 ms** vs SB-3.0 **117.9 ms** (~**40%** faster)
  - UKGOV year queries: ~**29.9%** / **279.26 ms** faster than SB-1.5; full-lifetime **931 ms** / **21%** faster
  — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Effect of cost-aware merging alone (IS -> RS-1000) on WIKI: day queries improve **62%**, month queries **35%**. On UKGOV: "does not show any considerable difference... due to the already low number of initial idealized shards" — i.e. the merging heuristic is self-organising — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Combining coalescing with partitioning: with epsilon=0.01, index size falls "**up to an order of magnitude for UKGOV and up to a factor of 2 for Wikipedia**", and sharded indexes are always smaller than sliced ones. Absolute index sizes plotted on axes of 0-20 GB (WIKI) and 0-10 GB (UKGOV) — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Build cost: "the time taken to build a sharded index is roughly **twice** the time taken for the standard unpartitioned inverted index" (but trivially parallelisable) — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- Query processing over a sharded index is cheaper to *merge* than over a sliced one: "merging results from every shard of an index list is not expensive since the shards are disjoint; this is in contrast to the situation for temporally sliced index lists, where entries replicated in different slices must be detected and" removed — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- There is a follow-up on incremental maintenance: Anand, Bedathur, Berberich, Schenkel, "Index maintenance for time-travel text search", SIGIR 2012, pp. 235-244, DOI 10.1145/2348283.2348318 — [OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1145%2F2348283.2348318)

### Inferences
- "Eager vs lazy materialisation" is not the vocabulary this literature uses. The actual axis is **replicate-into-time-slices (eager, 2-3x space, workload-tuned)** vs **disjoint docid shards + an in-memory impact list (lazy, ~1% space, storage-tuned)**. If you want the eager/lazy framing: slicing eagerly materialises a per-interval sublist; sharding materialises only an offset table and does the time filtering during the scan.
- The entire sharding benefit is a disk-seek argument: one random access plus a bounded sequential scan, versus scanning replicated entries. Both the 2-3x slicing cost and the 62% merging gains are I/O artefacts. On an in-memory or page-cached index the argument largely evaporates.
- Slicing's fatal practical property is that its optimum depends on the *query workload's* interval lengths: SB-3.0 wins at day granularity and loses at year/full-lifetime granularity, and vice versa for SB-1.5. A single index cannot be good at both. Sharding has no such dependence.

### Gaps
- I could not read the SIGIR 2012 index-maintenance paper's abstract or results (the webis anthology URL 404s, ACM DL is paywalled, the MPI host is down). Its existence and bibliographic details are confirmed; its approach and numbers are not.
- The 2011 report's index-size figures are presented only as bar charts (Figure 8.5) in the PDF; I recovered the axis ranges (0-20 GB WIKI, 0-10 GB UKGOV) but not the per-configuration GB values.

## The ranking-statistics problem: idf and collection size "as of" T

### Takeaway
The literature gives **two** real answers, and they are at opposite ends of the cost curve. (1) **Berberich et al. (I2T2, 2007): materialise a per-term idf *time series*, recomputed collection-wide at regular intervals over only the documents alive at that instant, and keep tf and idf as separate index components** — explicitly breaking the standard practice of storing their product. (2) **Staudinger, Piroi, Rauber (SIGIR-AP 2024): store `valid_from`/`valid_to` per document in a column store and compute df, N and avgdl on the fly at retrieval time from the as-of-T filtered document table**, accepting a ~25x-250x latency penalty. Nobody in the retrieved literature advocates accepting global statistics as an approximation, and I found **no paper that measures the ranking error from doing so** — that is the clearest gap here.

### Cited Findings
- **The statistics are formally time-varying.** BTW 2007 defines: `N(t)` = collection size at time t, `avdl(t)` = average document length, `df(v,t)` = number of documents containing term v at time t, "both computed over only the versions that are valid at time t". Both the tf-idf and Okapi BM25 models "require slight adaption, since statistical values such as document frequencies df and the collection size avdl are fixed in their original formulations and not time-varying as in our scenario" — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- **The formula**: `idf(v,t) = log( N(t) / (1 + df(v,t)) )`. For a query over a time *window* `[t_b, t_e]` (not a point), they aggregate: "we consider `idf(v,[t_b,t_e])` obtained by a simple **averaging** of idf values in the time-window, although other forms of aggregation are also applicable" — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- **The architectural decision**: "the term-document-specific tf-component and term-collection-specific idf-component are maintained **separately**, which is in contrast to common practice for static document collections where only their **product** is kept. In our setup, however, this separation adds the flexibility of computing the idf-component depending on the temporal context of the query." I2T2 therefore has three components: (1) **Versions** — the sequence of version timestamps per document, as a B-tree, used to derive each version's validity interval; (2) **IDF** — the time series of idf scores per term; (3) **TF** — the time series of tf scores per occurring term per document — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- **How the idf time series is built and queried**: "We maintain for each term the sequence of its idf-scores along with the associated validity time-interval. These idf-scores are **computed collection-wide for all terms at regular intervals** by taking into account **only the active documents**. At query time we scan this idf-score sequence for all query terms and retrieve the entries that are valid for the specified query time-window." In the experiments the interval was **monthly** — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- The authors note the asymmetry of cost: "Most crucial for the efficiency of query processing, however, is the way per-term per-document information is kept, since... accessing this pool contributes most of the query-processing cost" — i.e. the idf time series is cheap, the tf postings are the problem — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- The VLDB demo paper is candid that idf management was out of scope: it adopts Okapi BM25, notes that "idf-scores are also time-varying in this setting", and states that it does not detail their management, focusing on tf-scores — [VLDB 2007 demo](https://www.vldb.org/conf/2007/papers/demo/p1414-berberich.pdf)
- **The 2024 on-the-fly approach.** Problem statement: "as global term statistics change when documents change or are added to a corpus, queries using typical ranked retrieval models are not even reproducible for the parts of the document corpus that have **not** changed. Thus, Boolean retrieval frequently remains the mechanism of choice in such settings" — [Staudinger, Piroi, Rauber, "Reproducible Hybrid Time-Travel Retrieval in Evolving Corpora", SIGIR-AP 2024, DOI 10.1145/3673791.3698421](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04051)
- Their schema: the MonetDB column store's `Documents` table is extended "with two timestamps: `valid_from` and `valid_to`. This allows us to compute the term and document statistics that were valid at a given point in time and allow document versioning." Tables: `Dictionary(term_id, term)`, `Terms(term_id, document_id, term_frequency)`, `Documents(document_id, name, length, valid_from, valid_to)` — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- **The explicit rejection of precomputed df**: "an important difference... is that the **df value cannot be pre-computed and stored alongside the dictionary terms**, as the underlying collection evolves over time. Therefore, we **calculate the df at retrieval time** for VCBR, and do not keep any pre-computed values in the database" — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- Their as-of-T BM25 in SQL (k1=1.2, b=0.75): `N` = `SELECT count(document_id) FROM Documents WHERE added <= <ts> AND (removed IS NULL OR removed > <ts>)`; `avg_len` = `SELECT avg(len) FROM Documents WHERE added <= <ts> AND (removed IS NULL OR removed > <ts>)`; the document set is `SELECT ... FROM Documents WHERE valid_from <= <ts> AND (valid_to > <ts> OR valid_to IS NULL)`. They deliberately use Lucene's **approximated** document length (`intToByte4` quantisation) rather than exact length, to match Lucene's scores — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- Their measured cost: corpus = first **520,000** articles of the German Wikipedia May-2020 dump (**3.72 GB**), inserted in **26 batches of 20,000**, vocabulary **7,787,028** unique terms, **159,150,478** total terms. Storage: MonetDB **4.05 GB** (Dict 194 MB, Docs 39 MB, Terms 3.82 GB) alongside a Lucene index of **3.82 GB** — "the hybrid system thus more or less **doubles** the storage requirements for the index structures". Latency: Lucene ~**80 ms/query** and flat; MonetDB **linear in corpus size, from ~2000 ms initially to ~20 seconds** against 520,000 documents. Indexing: Lucene ~13 min per 20k docs (flat); MonetDB transfer overhead grows 2:30 min -> 8 min per batch — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- They judge that latency acceptable for its purpose: "while such a performance degradation would be prohibitive in a live query processing system, it is acceptable for re-execution/reproducibility studies... Also note, that no optimization such as sharding of database tables across multiple nodes has been applied" — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- Measured *agreement* between the two scoring paths (not a time-local-vs-global measurement, but the only numeric ranking-difference data I found): score differences between Lucene and the column store are all "in the range of **1e-5**", while the score gap between consecutive documents in a ranked list is of order **1e-2**, so both systems return identical ranked lists; over **26 x 400 = 10,400** queries exactly **one** case occurred where two documents were close enough to swap (they quote ~**1 in 10,000**), detected by a result-set hash mismatch and repaired by swapping the single candidate pair — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- Query workload for that measurement: four sets of 100 queries of 1, 2, 5 and 10 terms, BM25, top-20 — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- A third, *orthogonal* answer exists in the compression literature: He, Zeng and Suel explicitly decline to take a position, evaluating "our index structures by running queries over **all versions, with no temporal restrictions**", and noting that restricting a query to a time interval "could be achieved either by **post-filtering**, or through specialized index structures that support more efficient range queries over time" — [He, Zeng, Suel, "Improved Index Compression Techniques for Versioned Document Collections", CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)

### Inferences
- The two published answers bracket your design space, and the deciding variable is **how often T changes**. Berberich's materialised idf time series is right when as-of queries cluster on a modest number of timepoints (he used monthly granularity, which also *defines* the achievable temporal resolution of idf). The 2024 on-the-fly SQL is right when T is arbitrary and latency is not user-facing.
- Berberich's scheme is itself an approximation with an unmeasured error: idf is sampled monthly, so an as-of query at an intra-month timepoint gets the month's idf, not the true instantaneous one. Nobody quantifies that error. Likewise the averaging of idf over a query window is an unjustified choice the paper flags as arbitrary ("other forms of aggregation are also applicable").
- The separation of tf and idf is the single most transferable design lesson, and it has a second payoff noted in the first section: an unnormalised tf payload coalesces far better than a BM25-normalised one. Both arguments push the same way — **keep raw tf in the posting, keep N(T)/df(v,T)/avgdl(T) outside it.**
- The 2024 paper's framing is the cleanest statement of *why* this matters even when you do not care about history: if statistics are global, then adding documents silently reranks results for documents that did not change. If you want "the answer I got in March" to be reproducible in October, you need time-local statistics even for a query about unchanged documents.
- Their 2000 ms figure at the first 20,000-document batch is the only published latency datapoint anywhere near a small corpus, and it is bad — but it is a naive single-node SQL BM25 over a column store with no index on the join, not evidence that computing df-at-T is intrinsically expensive. A `df` computed by intersecting an FTS posting list with a bitmap of documents alive at T is a different cost model entirely.

### Gaps
- **No paper I could find measures the ranking error from substituting global (current-snapshot) statistics for time-local statistics.** The Berberich papers measure the error from approximating *tf* (coalescing epsilon: tau ~0.8, 75% top-100 overlap at 16% index size). The 2024 paper measures *implementation* agreement (1e-5 score deltas, 1 swap in 10,400 queries) and argues the problem qualitatively. Neither is the experiment you want: run the same as-of-T query with (a) df/N from T and (b) df/N from "now", and report nDCG/RBO/Kendall differences. I found no such study; if the report asserts a magnitude, it must be flagged as unmeasured in the literature.
- No source quantifies how much of the total index size the idf time series itself consumes in I2T2.
- I found no treatment of time-varying `avgdl` beyond its definition `avdl(t)` and the 2024 paper's `avg(len)` subquery — no one reports how much BM25's length normalisation drifts with corpus age.

## Related practice: web archives, Wikipedia revisions, code search

### Takeaway
Almost nothing in production does as-of-T *ranked full-text* search. Web archives ship **URL search with chronological (timestamp-ordered) version lists** plus a separate, much smaller full-text index over a *snapshot-like* view, built on stock Lucene/Solr/Elasticsearch with ordinary global statistics. Code search systems (Zoekt/Sourcegraph) index a bounded set of **named branches** with per-file branch bitmasks and deduplicate identical blobs — they do not index commit history at all, and their ranking is heuristic, not BM25-over-a-time-slice.

### Cited Findings
- Web archive discovery reality: "URL search is the prevalent discovery method in web archives"; it "matches all archived versions of a given URL and its possible aliases"; "the matching versions are traditionally presented in a chronological view... the versions are ranked by their **timestamp**" — [Miguel Costa, "Full-text and URL search over web archives", arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- Web archives build on commodity IR stacks: Lucene "and its extensions for distributed processing, such as Solr, ElasticSearch" — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- Ranking features used are the standard web-search set (TF-IDF and BM25, term proximity, PageRank, anchor text), combined with learning-to-rank; "creating precise ranking models for a new type of search, such as for web archives" is identified as an open problem — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- **Users barely use the time filter**: "There is strong evidence that users prefer the oldest documents over the newest, but **mostly do not search with any temporal restriction** or use temporal expressions. This is surprising, since all information needs are focused on the past and not even large time intervals were used to narrow queries." And: web archive users "said they wanted to see the evolution of a page over time, but they tended to click on just **one or two versions of each URL**" — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- Scalability workarounds actually proposed for archive full-text search: index **only anchor texts**, "which are many orders of magnitude smaller" (Holzmann et al. 2017); or outsource ranking to a commercial engine (Bing) over the live web and link results to the Wayback Machine (Kanhabua et al. 2016), with the noted downside that "web search engines favour fresh over old content, which does not apply to web archives" — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- A 2021 statement of the Internet Archive's full-text status: "full-text search is only available in beta mode for a few million documents. Currently, URL search is the main method to find information in the Internet Archive" — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603)
- OpenWayback architecture separates a **Resource Store** from a **Resource Index**, where "the Resource Index handles queries against the documents located in the Resource Store, either through URL based queries, full-text search, or other novel search mechanisms" — [OpenWayback architecture overview wiki](https://github.com/iipc/openwayback/wiki/Architecture-overview)
- UK Web Archive: the `webarchive-discovery` tool parses ARC/WARC files and indexes them into **Apache Solr** (fields include title, host, crawl-date, content type); the Shine front-end indexed **3.5 billion resources**, described as less than a third of holdings, with a need to scale past ten billion documents; Shine development largely stalled after the BUDDAH project ended. **Warclight** (a Rails/Blacklight engine) is the successor over the same Solr schema — [Andy Jackson (UKWA), "Discovery and access plans"](https://anjackson.net/2017/06/09/discovery-and-access-plans/); [Warclight, YorkSpace](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/items/fa50bf91-fa0e-4ed3-b6f9-120dd4c4f8e8)
- Wikipedia revision history as an indexing target: **Hedera** is a MapReduce pipeline for indexing Wikipedia revision history into a full-text index and extracting entity information — [Tran and Nguyen, "Hedera: Scalable Indexing and Exploring Entities in Wikipedia Revision History", arXiv:1701.03937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1701.03937)
- **Zoekt** (the engine behind Sourcegraph, originally by Han-Wen Nienhuys ex-Google): each indexed file blob carries "a bitmask, representing the branches in which the content is found", so "we can index many similar branches of a repository with little space overhead". The design doc **does not mention indexing commit history** — only branch content. Index size: about **3x the corpus size** (~2x for offsets, ~1x for the original content); shard size also ~3x corpus; positional trigram search needs ~**1.2x corpus in RAM**; uint32 offsets cap a shard below 4 GB, i.e. ~1 GB of content per shard — [Zoekt design.md](https://gerrit.googlesource.com/zoekt/+show/0314dee638e2b18be32ff3cc81efc2d4d4fc9f2f/doc/design.md)
- Zoekt's ranking signals, as proposed in the design doc: number of query atoms matched, proximity of matches for different atoms, match on word boundaries, **file last-updated time**, filename length, match inside a comment/string literal, match is a symbol definition (via ctags). No BM25, no collection statistics — [Zoekt design.md](https://gerrit.googlesource.com/zoekt/+show/0314dee638e2b18be32ff3cc81efc2d4d4fc9f2f/doc/design.md)
- Sourcegraph: "the indexer will deduplicate documents between branches", so "the size of your index will grow in relation to the number of **unique** documents"; up to **64 branches** per repository may be indexed, with extra branches adding resource requirements (particularly memory) — [Sourcegraph code search features docs](https://sourcegraph.com/docs/code-search/features.md)
- Sourcegraph's indexed-ranking page lists, among Zoekt's heuristics, that files appearing on **more branches** are treated as more important, and that the ranking primarily "is used to decide the order we search the documents, so is most important when hitting limits" — [Sourcegraph 4.5 indexed ranking](https://4.5.sourcegraph.com/dev/background-information/architecture/indexed-ranking)

### Inferences
- The web-archive answer to the ranking-statistics problem is to **not have the problem**: full-text search runs over a single conflated index with global statistics, and "time travel" is delivered by URL lookup plus a timestamp-ordered version list. No archive system I found claims time-local idf.
- Code search converged on exactly the structure Zoekt describes: a *set* of named revisions (branches), content-addressed dedup, and a bitmask per blob saying which revisions contain it. That is a bitmap-per-version-set design, and it is the closest shipped analogue to an as-of-T filter — but it scales to tens of revisions, not thousands of commits, and the 64-branch ceiling is where that design runs out.
- Costa's user finding (people do not use temporal restrictions; they click one or two versions per URL) is the strongest external evidence that an as-of-T ranked search is a niche capability even where it would be most useful. For a small corpus, that argues for correctness-and-simplicity over any of the performance machinery in the first two sections.

### Gaps
- I found **no public description of the Internet Archive's current full-text index internals** (postings layout, whether captures are deduplicated, how scoring treats multiple captures of a URL). The search surfaced secondary claims (a figure of 510+ billion captures attributed to blog.archive.org; a keyword-search index built from 400+ billion hyperlinks to site homepages, ranked by inlink count and total captures per site, attributed to coverage of the 2016 keyword-search launch) that I could not verify against the primary pages within budget. These should be treated as unverified and re-checked before use.
- I found nothing on Common Crawl shipping a full-text time-travel index (Common Crawl publishes per-crawl WARC/columnar index files; there is no evidence of a cross-crawl as-of-T ranked search service).
- I found no documentation of as-of-T full-text search over git history in Google's internal Code Search, Hound, or Sourcegraph. Sourcegraph/Zoekt document commit-diff and commit-message search, not an index you can query as of an arbitrary past commit.

## Deduplication across versions, and how much of a corpus actually changes

### Takeaway
The best-measured corpus-change statistics in this literature are from He, Zeng and Suel (CIKM 2010): **at least half of all consecutive version pairs differ by fewer than 5 unique terms added or removed**, and **fewer than 10% of versions account for more than 50% (Wikipedia) and 70% (Internet Archive Irish domain) of all change**. Change is also bursty in time. This is the entire basis for versioned-index compression, and its payoff is proportional to versions-per-document.

### Cited Findings
- Datasets: **Wiki** = 0.24 million distinct documents with **35 versions/doc on average**, "slightly more than 8 million pages", about 10% of English Wikipedia. **Ireland** = 1.06 million documents from the Irish web domain collected by the Internet Archive between 1996 and 2006, with **15 versions/doc on average** — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Property 1 ("most changes are small"): "at least half of all versions in Wiki and Ireland differ by **less than 5 terms** that are added or removed". Caveat stated by the authors: this is a set-based model over unique terms, a change size of 0 means the same term *set*, and all identical versions were already removed from the data — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Property 2 ("term changes are bursty"): "the longer a term stays unchanged, the more unlikely it is to change in the next step. Conversely, terms that have just been added or removed are more likely to disappear or reappear again in the next few steps" — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Property 3 ("change size is bursty"): "less than **10%** of all versions are responsible for more than **50% (Wiki)** and **70% (Ireland)** of the total change. So, large changes are rare, but account for much of the total change." (They note the distribution does not appear to follow a power law.) — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Why this matters for the index: small inter-version change is "the main reason why a versioned index structure should be much smaller than a standard structure that treats each version as a separate document. All existing techniques for versioned collections exploit this property" — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Named techniques and measured index sizes (MB, same datasets, directly comparable to prior work): one-level vs two-level, DIFF (store changes from previous versions) and MSA (multiple-sequence-alignment style), with IPC and PFD (PForDelta) compression:
  - DIFF-IPC: 269 (Wiki) / 751 (Ireland) one-level -> **253 / 652** two-level
  - DIFF-PFD: 323 / 927 -> 269 / 757
  - MSA-IPC: 237 / 682 -> **233 / 663**
  - MSA-PFD: 287 / 799 -> 243 / 672
  — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010, Table 3](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Combinatorial bounds on achievable size (MB), from their analysis of the three change models (the lower bound is a worst-case bound for a class of inputs, not for these datasets): Wiki upper/lower 117/114, 92/89, 92/88; Ireland 295/288, 267/261, 254/249 — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010, Table 1](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- End-to-end query cost on Wiki, fully in memory, 10,000 queries sampled from the AOL log restricted to queries that produced a Wikipedia click (ms/query; index MB; decoded docIDs per query):
  - Sorted (previous fastest): **4.28 (IPC) / 0.97 (PFD)** ms; 570 / 583 MB; **245** decoded postings
  - 2-DIFF: 1.34 / **0.66** / 0.68 ms; 253 / 294 / 269 MB; **34** postings
  - 2R-DIFF: 1.51 / 0.83 / 0.85 ms; **176** / 217 / 193 MB; 34 postings
  - 2-MSA: 1.68 / 0.99 / 1.01 ms; 233 / 274 / 243 MB; **28** postings
  - 2R-MSA: 1.68 / 0.99 / 1.01 ms; 179 / 220 / 195 MB; 28 postings
  - 2-Hybrid: 1.59 / 0.88 / 0.90 ms; **166** / 207 / 182 MB; 30 postings
  - 2R-HUFF: > 2 ms (Huffman decoding cost)
  — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010, Table 6](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- The dominant mechanism behind the speedup is reading less, not decoding faster: the two-level methods decode **28-34** postings per query where Sorted decodes **245** — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- Naive cost of ignoring versioning: "for a collection with 50 versions per document" treating each version as a separate document is wasteful, blowing up the index proportionally "even if many versions are almost identical" — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- A distinct dedup mechanism in production code search: content-addressed blobs with a branch bitmask, so content shared by several branches is stored once ("the indexer will deduplicate documents between branches... the size of your index will grow in relation to the number of unique documents") — [Sourcegraph docs](https://sourcegraph.com/docs/code-search/features.md); [Zoekt design.md](https://gerrit.googlesource.com/zoekt/+show/0314dee638e2b18be32ff3cc81efc2d4d4fc9f2f/doc/design.md)
- Related direction the authors distinguish: exploiting similarity between *distinct* documents (docid reordering, clustering) is a related but different problem — "similarities between distinct documents are typically smaller, leading to more limited gains, and... an important part of the problem is to identify which documents are similar, while in versioned collections this information is more or less implied" — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)

### Inferences
- Both the compression literature (DIFF/MSA/HUFF, 35 and 15 versions/doc) and the coalescing literature (3.13 to 25.23 versions/doc) earn their keep from *version multiplicity*. The ceiling on what any of these techniques can save is roughly the mean versions-per-document: there is no redundancy to squeeze out of a document with one version.
- The "fewer than 10% of versions carry 50-70% of the change" finding is the most useful operational fact here: a change-size-aware policy (keep full postings for the rare big edits, delta/coalesce the rest) is where the headroom is. The CIKM 2010 paper says explicitly that no prior technique exploited this property and that their gains rest on it.

### Gaps
- No source I found reports the fraction of *documents* (as opposed to versions) that change between two crawls/snapshots of a corpus — the statistics are all per-consecutive-version-pair.
- Nobody in this set reports deduplication ratios in the storage sense (identical-blob dedup rates); He/Zeng/Suel had already removed byte-identical versions from their datasets before measuring, which means their "at least half differ by <5 terms" figure *excludes* no-op versions and understates real-corpus redundancy.

## Time-as-filter vs recency-as-ranking-signal

### Takeaway
They are formally different problems: time-travel search makes time a **hard predicate on which versions are candidates** (and therefore a question about what the collection statistics *were*), while temporal IR makes time a **feature in the scoring function over a current collection**. The time-travel literature says so explicitly, and the one hybrid idea it floats — relaxing the temporal constraint into a preference — is named as future work, not done.

### Cited Findings
- Time-travel semantics are a hard filter: a time-travel query "retrieves the set of document versions that satisfy the keyword query Q **and existed at any time during** the time interval", formalised as `R(Q@[b,e]) = { d_k : all q in Q are in d_k AND I(d_k) intersect [b,e] != empty }`; the degenerate single-timepoint case is called a **time-point query** — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- The motivating example is explicitly about suppressing recency: for the query `inheritance tax @[2000-2002]`, "the same query **without** a temporal constraint would tend to generate results only about latest taxation details and not necessarily from the required time" — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- The hybrid is named as future work: "another interesting direction we plan to explore is to automatically **relax the temporal context** in time-travel queries, thus treating it rather as a **preference parameter** of the user than as a **hard condition** for filtering results" — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- The genuinely time-aware-*ranking* line, for contrast: "multiple models trained and optimised for specific time periods showed better performance than using just one single model to all data (Costa et al., 2014)" — i.e. time conditions the *model*, not the candidate set — [Costa, arXiv:2108.01603](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.01603); primary citation: Costa, Couto, Silva, "Learning temporal-dependent ranking models", SIGIR 2014
- A third, intermediate formulation: **durable search**, "where the goal is to return documents consistently ranked high over a range of time" — related to time-travel but asking for stability across a window rather than a snapshot — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- A separate use of "temporal idf" that is *not* about as-of reconstruction: letting document frequencies decay over time for novelty detection in news streams — [arXiv:1401.1456, "Using temporal IDF for efficient novelty detection in text streams"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.1456)

### Inferences
- The distinction has a concrete consequence for implementation, not just taxonomy: in recency-as-a-feature IR, the collection is a single current snapshot, so `df` and `N` are unambiguous and global statistics are *correct*. The moment time becomes a filter, `df` and `N` become functions of T, and the standard precomputed-statistics shortcut becomes an approximation whose error nobody has measured. Every technique in this survey exists because of that one change.
- "Durable search" and the BTW "relax the temporal context" note are the two places where the window-aggregation question (Berberich's averaging of idf over `[t_b, t_e]`, and the MAX/MIN/TAVG version-score aggregation) becomes unavoidable. For a pure time-*point* query, none of that aggregation machinery is needed — a simplification worth taking if your query shape is "as of T" rather than "during [T1,T2]".

### Gaps
- I did not pursue the large temporal-IR / recency-ranking literature (out of scope per the assignment); the two citations above are illustrative, not a survey of it.

## Scale calibration for a 10,500-document / 14,225-version corpus

### Takeaway
At ~**1.35 versions per document**, essentially every space-saving technique in this literature is inapplicable, because they all monetise version multiplicity that this corpus does not have. The ranking-statistics problem, by contrast, is **scale-free** — it is a correctness question, and it is the one finding from this literature that transfers directly.

### Cited Findings
Corpus sizes in the primary sources, for direct comparison (all cited above):
- BTW 2007 / I2T2: 892,255 docs, **2,795,383 versions**, 3.13 versions/doc — [BTW 2007](https://dl.gi.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/89bdf0ed-c5bc-44e4-ad41-376676593384/content)
- SIGIR 2011 sharding, WIKI: 1,517,524 docs, **15,079,829 versions**, 9.94 versions/doc, 0.7 TB raw; UKGOV: 685,678 docs, **17,297,548 versions**, 25.23 versions/doc, 0.4 TB — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)
- CIKM 2010 compression, Wiki: 0.24M docs x **35 versions/doc** (~8M pages); Ireland: 1.06M docs x **15 versions/doc** — [He, Zeng, Suel, CIKM 2010](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/version.pdf)
- SIGIR-AP 2024 (the smallest corpus in the set): **520,000 documents**, 3.72 GB, with the MonetDB as-of-T path already at ~2000 ms/query at its **first 20,000-document batch** and ~20 s at 520,000 — [arXiv 2411.04051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04051)
- The dividing line for slicing is stated as an I/O argument: sharding wins because "a random access is at least as expensive as a sequential read as in disk- and network-based index storage" — [MPI-I-2011-5-001](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2111663_1/component/file_2111662/content)

### Inferences
Explicit "does not pay off at this scale" calls:
- **Approximate temporal coalescing**: pays off in proportion to versions/doc. The published 6x reduction came from a corpus with 3.13 versions/doc where accurate (lossless) coalescing alone recovered only ~9%. At 1.35 versions/doc the naive per-version posting blow-up ceiling is ~1.35x, so the absolute saving is at most ~26% of index size — and it costs you tau ~0.8 ranking fidelity to get there. **Not worth it.** Keep accurate (exact-payload) coalescing if it is free; skip epsilon.
- **Temporal slicing / sublist materialisation (SB-gamma)**: buys scan-length reductions at 2-3x space on multi-GB posting lists. This corpus's posting lists will not be long enough for a seek to beat a scan. **Not worth it.**
- **Temporal index sharding (staircase + cost-aware merging)**: the entire gain is eliminating random accesses on disk-resident lists; measured wins were 10-70 ms on lists averaging 0.5-0.9M entries. At 14,225 versions the whole index is page-cache resident. **Not worth it.**
- **DIFF/MSA/HUFF two-level versioned compression**: designed for 15-35 versions/doc, where bit-vectors per term across versions have enough length to have structure; the authors themselves observe weaker gains on the 15-version dataset than the 35-version one because "shorter bit vectors offer less structure that can be exploited". At 1.35 versions/doc there is no structure. **Not worth it.**
- **Branch-bitmask dedup (Zoekt-style)**: this one *does* transfer, because it is cheap and version-count-independent — a per-posting bitmap or interval of "which versions contain this" is the same idea as a valid-time interval, and at 14,225 versions a roaring-bitmap-per-term-per-document is tractable where it would not be at 17M.
- **Time-local collection statistics**: transfers fully, and is the only thing in this literature that gets *worse* relative to the alternatives as the corpus shrinks. `df(v,T)` on a 10,500-document corpus has small counts, so a single document appearing or disappearing moves `log(N/df)` materially; the global-statistics approximation is *less* defensible at this scale, not more. The counting is also cheap: an as-of-T document bitmap of 14,225 bits is ~1.8 KB, and `df(v,T)` is a popcount of (posting-list bitmap AND alive-at-T bitmap).
- On the 2024 paper's architecture choice: their hybrid pays double storage and 25x-250x latency for exactness because their statistics path is unindexed SQL aggregation. At 10,500 documents the materialise-vs-compute question largely dissolves — you can afford to compute `N(T)`, `avgdl(T)` and `df(v,T)` per query *and* to cache them per distinct T, which is Berberich's materialised idf time series arrived at from the other direction.

### Gaps
- No source in this literature reports results on a corpus anywhere near 10^4 documents, so there is no published latency or quality datapoint to extrapolate from at this scale. All of the above are inferences from the mechanisms, not measured transfers.
