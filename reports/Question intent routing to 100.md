# Evidence closes two questions, not eight

Of the eight questions left open on Episode 6's time set, exactly **two (G-069, G-070) close on measured internal evidence**, one (G-058) has a plausible mechanism that has never been run, and **five (G-067, G-071, G-051, G-055, G-074) have no mechanism in hand that is derivable from the schema** — their evidence is already in the right haystack and loses on score, which is the textbook configuration for fitting. The mechanism that produces the two closes is honest: a single state-boundary predicate over the unit-state table, whose candidate sets shrink **9,537→42, 4,454→20, 11,492→517** with the expected address verified present in all five windows (measured, internal), lifting reranked Hit@4 from **0/5 unrestricted to 3/5** (measured, internal). It is a restriction, which is the shape that measures badly in the literature, and it is defensible only because its recall was verified rather than assumed. On the headline question: **"100%" cannot be claimed honestly as a statement about the system, and can be claimed honestly only as a statement about 71 specific strings.** A perfect 47/47 establishes a 95% one-sided lower bound of **93.8%**; 24/24 establishes **88.3%**; 13/13 establishes **79.4%** ([rule of three, 0.05^(1/n)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10811) applied at these n). Three one-way flips give an exact two-sided sign-test **p=0.250**, and **six clean wins with zero regressions** are the minimum for p<0.05 — so three of the eight questions are, by arithmetic, incapable of ever constituting a statistically significant improvement, and the whole eight-question gap is worth at most one significant result (p=0.0078 if all eight flip cleanly).

## 100% means 93.8%, and the gap is worth exactly one p-value

The confidence arithmetic is unforgiving at these sizes and should be published before any score is. A perfect score on the main set, **47/47, carries a 95% one-sided lower bound of 0.938**; the two-sided Wilson interval is [0.924, 1.000] and Clopper-Pearson [0.925, 1.000]. The time set's **24/24 bounds at 0.883**, the held-out **13/13 at 0.794**, and all 90 together at 0.967. Clopper-Pearson is the exact, guaranteed-coverage interval and Wilson the recommended closed form at small n and extreme p; Recht et al. note that an interval tightens to "at most ±1%" only at n=10,000, which is the scale at which a proportion becomes a measurement rather than a description ([Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10811)). One question is worth 4.17 percentage points at n=24 and 2.13 at n=47 — granularity, not precision, and a proportion over 24 questions can take only 25 distinct values, so bootstrapping it adds nothing over the exact binomial and will undercover.

Paired testing is where the eight-question gap meets its ceiling. Under an exact sign test or McNemar, only discordant questions count and n is irrelevant: three one-way flips is **p=0.250 two-sided (0.125 one-sided)**, four is 0.125, five is 0.0625, and **six is the first value under 0.05 (p=0.031)**. Allowing regressions, the minimum net wins for p<0.05 are 6 with zero regressions, 8 with one, 10 with two, 12 with three. Closing all eight cleanly gives p=0.0078; closing them across four episodes of two gives four results at p=0.50 each. Because Hit@4 is binary, a paired randomisation test — IR's standard and more powerful choice, since it uses per-query magnitudes — degenerates to the sign test, so these figures are the hard floor regardless of which test is chosen. The implication is strategic rather than statistical: **if a defensible statistical claim is wanted out of this work, the whole gap must be banked into one pre-registered confirmatory evaluation**, not spent two questions at a time.

Two further constraints bound what any of this can mean. First, the eval sets sit below every published size recommendation: the IR community's own floor is **25 topics minimum and 50 for a reliable evaluation**, while power analyses push to **150 topics** to distinguish one system from another, with required counts for individual system pairs ranging from about **10 to 722 topics** ([Cheap IR Evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00479); [Webber, Moffat & Zobel, CIKM 2008](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/abstracts/wmz08cikm.html)). The 47 answerable main questions sit just above the floor; the 24-question time set and 13-question held-out set are below it. The literature's advice when more topics are impossible is to judge **more topics shallowly rather than few deeply**, which translates directly: adding questions is worth more than deepening qrels on existing ones, and a set growing ~15 questions per episode reaches the 150-topic threshold in about seven episodes while a frozen 90 never does. Second, the adaptive-analysis budget is effectively spent. Solving the non-adaptive union bound `sqrt(log k / n) <= 1/n` — keeping bias under one question — gives **k ≤ 1.04 at n=24 and k ≤ 1.02 at n=47**: about **one look**. Allowing two questions of bias at n=24 still gives k ≤ 1.18. This figure is theory evaluated at these n, **not an empirical finding** — the note that produced it flags that no study measures adaptivity-driven overfitting at n<100, since both the theory (Ladder, reusable holdout) and the empirical work (ImageNet, CIFAR, MS MARCO) operate at n in the thousands. The worst-case bound is therefore uninformative at these sizes for any k≥2, and the only real protection is procedural: the no-tuning rule, pre-registration, and scoring the held-out 13 exactly once are not stylistic preferences, they are the entirety of the defence. The encouraging counterweight is that honest development has historically burned test sets far slower than the bound implies — years of ImageNet reuse left **ranking intact, with gains on the original set translating to larger gains on a fresh one** ([Recht et al., ICML 2019](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/recht19a)) — which shifts the operative question from "how many looks" to **"are my changes adversarial in form?"** A change derived from the data schema is not an attack; a change derived from inspecting which three questions failed is precisely a low-order boosting step of the kind the Ladder paper demonstrates ([arXiv:1502.04585](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04585)).

The ratchet deserves naming here because a reader will otherwise misread it. A rule that accepts only non-decreasing measurements makes the reported score **a running maximum over every candidate change evaluated**, including changes whose true effect is zero or negative but whose measured effect happened not to be negative. Over k candidates the measured score drifts upward by roughly the expected maximum of k noise draws — the same `sqrt(log k / n)` quantity, which at n=47 is 0.185 of the set (8.7 questions) at k=5 and 0.221 (10.4 questions) at k=10. **The ratchet is good engineering and zero evidence of improvement.** Its one clause with real teeth is "lose no more than one question in any stratum", because trading strata against each other to buy headline points is the characteristic signature of fitting. Its weakest clause is "stale@4 must not rise": with a stale base count of 1–2, "did not rise" is the overwhelmingly likely outcome under any change, good or bad — a tripwire for catastrophe, not a measurement, fixable only by counting stale hits across all 90 questions rather than within the stale stratum.

## Per question: two measured closes, one untested mechanism, five ranking losses

The what-changed group has real measurement behind it. The original `changed_between(lo, hi)` predicate returned haystacks of 109–4,434 addresses; a two-part *across*/*within* definition cut those to 13–459 with all five answers retained; and the research then found a false negative the two-part version structurally cannot see — **change-and-change-back (A→B→A)**, where the endpoint texts match although the address moved twice. The fix drops the endpoint-text comparison entirely in favour of **a state boundary strictly inside the window, partitioned on presence at the window start**, which is simpler, cheaper, and subsumes both halves. That single predicate also exposes the two classes the two-part version could not express, and the first of them is the project's thesis as a number: **`born-once` — created inside the window and never touched again — is 4–10× larger than the set that actually moved** (211 vs 42, 361 vs 20, 3,598 vs 517; measured, internal). "The story of a change outranks the change" is no longer a phrase.

Reranked with Episode 5's own reader (`nomic-embed-text` + `ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2`, same depth throughout), the five what-changed windows score **Hit@4 0/5 unrestricted, 1/5 under `began`, 3/5 under `boundary`** (measured, internal) — and the third of those three is G-068, which was already passing, so **the boundary predicate closes two of the eight**. Two details in that table deserve to be published rather than smoothed. G-069 and G-070 both land at exactly rank 4, so neither is a robust close; a reader change moves them out. And G-071 presents a contradiction across the two internal measurement passes: without a reranker it sat at **rank 5** under the old `changed_between` restriction, and with the cross-encoder it sits at **rank 15** under the much tighter boundary restriction. The two passes used different readers and are therefore not a controlled comparison, but the arithmetic suggests — **this is an inference, not a measurement** — that the cross-encoder is demoting G-071's evidence, and that the honest next experiment for that one question is a reader ablation, not another restriction.

The history and as-known group is where the eight-question framing becomes dangerous. **Three of the eight (G-051, G-055, G-074) sit in a haystack that is already correct and lose on score**, and the evaluation arithmetic proves these can never constitute a statistically significant improvement at this n: three flips is p=0.250, and that is a property of the effect size, not a sample-size problem that more main-set questions would fix. Fixing them by adjusting scoring weights, thresholds or boosts chosen with reference to those three questions is a boosting step in the Ladder's sense — a change with no model behind it that raises the measured score while true loss stays at chance ([arXiv:1502.04585](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04585)). The defences, in order of strength, are: derive the scoring change from the schema and accept whatever it does to those three, including nothing; require the fix to fire on at least ~3× its target count with zero harm, because a mechanism aimed at three questions that fires on exactly three is a lookup table wearing a function's clothes; or leave them failing and report them. **An episode that ends at 45/47 with a published explanation of the residual ranking losses is a stronger artefact than one that ends at 47/47 with an unexplained scoring tweak**, and far stronger than one where a reader cannot tell which happened.

Two structural gaps surround this set rather than sitting inside it. First, `repealed` — in force at the window start, gone by the end — is **0 in all five windows** (measured, internal), so the mechanism is a *substitution* detector and the time set contains no question that would warn us; a repeal question would fail silently. Legislative practice treats change as a three-way taxonomy — insertion, repeal, substitution ([legislation.gov.uk timeline keying](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/46/section/790T/2023-12-01); [Spinosa et al.](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/1330)) — so this is a recall hole, not an edge case. Second, **release-tag windows are pre-resolved in the golden set** (`from_ref: "v0.1.0-pre.27"` carries its timestamp), which means the set performs a resolution step a production retriever would have to do itself, and **that step has never been measured** (measured, internal). A 24/24 on the time set is therefore a score on a slightly easier task than the one the system claims to do.

## Expansion and content are safe; restriction and weights are bets

The routing research resolves into two asymmetries that sort every candidate mechanism cleanly. **Expansion is safe and restriction is not**: expansion can only add recall, while the one placebo-controlled study of document structure measures naive two-stage hierarchical retrieval at **−0.033 / −0.015 cov-nDCG@10**, traced to poor first-stage section recall ([Kuehlkamp et al., alphaXiv 2610.10170](https://alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.10170v1)), and a vendor benchmark shows broad-value filters **lowering recall substantially while queries still return results and do not look broken** ([Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/blog/pre-filtering-vs-post-filtering/index.md)). **Content is safe and weights are not**: real heading paths beat a semantically null placebo of identical form by **+0.010 (Wikipedia) / +0.016 (QASPER)** cov-nDCG@10, Holm-corrected and consistent in direction across both corpora, while every weighting scheme in the literature needs labelled queries — BM25F with **uniform, unfitted weights is worse than plain BM25** (Amazon Hit@1 **0.183 vs 0.483**), and mFAR's query-conditioned gate, the strongest measured result in the family, was trained on **6,000–8,000 queries** and loses most of its benefit when query conditioning is removed (Hit@1 **0.437→0.338**) ([mFAR, arXiv 2410.20056](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20056v2)).

## The classifier question: the research points at a cascade, not a classifier

The instinct to replace the hand-written regex lists with a model-based classifier is **half right, and the half that is wrong is the more expensive half**. Start with what the incumbent actually scores, because no published number is as relevant. Three English lists that were never measured as classifiers come out at: `WHY` → `#context` **21% precision** on 19 firings (its own comment already said 4/19 and called it "poor precision on purpose"); `HISTORY_MARKERS` **25% precision, 62% recall** on 20 firings; and the `DOC_KINDS` table carrying **two rows that have never been right once** — `triage log` fires twice with neither question wanting it and misses all five that do, and `plan` fires three times with none wanting it and misses the one that does, **because the file was renamed to `design.html` on 30 July 2026 and the row still names a path that no longer exists** (all measured, internal). Scored as it is actually used, `_named_kind` **names a document on 27 of 90 questions and is right on 11 — precision 41% — and stays silent on 63, including 54 whose evidence sits in exactly one document**. It is wrong more often than right when it commits. The architecture survives this because the routed result is fused and never committed (`scope_rrf`), at the cost that **no measurement can distinguish "the scope arm was not needed" from "the scope arm asked for a document that does not exist at that position"**.

Against that incumbent, the literature's rule-vs-learned gains are the wrong yardstick. A "carefully designed" rule baseline reaches **~75% accuracy / F 0.40** against a CNN's **~81.6% / 0.50**, and on a second dataset the rule baseline scores 77% accuracy with **precision 0.45, recall 0.34** ([QRUMS 2016](https://people.cs.pitt.edu/~hashemi/papers/QRUMS2016_HBHashemi.pdf)); production search studies report **+4.14% (CNN) and +7.11% (LSTM)** over a production *linear* baseline ([arXiv 2008.06759](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.06759)). Our lists sit at 0.21–0.25 precision — **below a competent rule baseline, not below a learned one** — so the comparison the ratchet needs is **three-way: current regexes, repaired regexes, model classifier**. Some of the deficit is plain list quality (`\bwhy\b` fires on "why does X return Y", a behaviour question, not a rationale question), and the literature has **no measurement of how much of a regex router's error is fixable by improving the regexes versus inherent to lexical matching** — so the repaired-regex arm is cheap, under-explored, and cannot be skipped without leaving the model's gain unattributable.

Where the research genuinely supports the model is the **none-class and schema agility, not accuracy**. The incumbent's failure is 15 false positives in 19 fires — an out-of-scope problem — and that is exactly where zero-shot LLMs win decisively: **OOS F1 85.0 and OOS recall 85.6% for Claude zero-shot, against 73.0 / 58.1% for a fine-tuned RoBERTa and 51.7 / 36.4% for TF-IDF+LR**, achieved by simply allowing the model to answer `oos` with no threshold ([arXiv 2608.20371](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20371)). The same paper shows a RoBERTa trained on a 75-intent schema scoring **96.8 on that schema and 0.0 on a disjoint one**, while schema-prompted Claude scores 94.3 / 93.9 with no retraining — and a spec archive's section vocabulary will change. Where the research points **away** from the classifier is equally specific. On small label sets the trained classifier wins by a wide margin (**ATIS, 26 labels: RoBERTa 95.9 vs Claude zero-shot 84.1, gap 11.8 points, CI [9.2, 14.4], McNemar p=2.5×10⁻¹⁶**) and only ties on large ones (**CLINC150, 150 labels: gap 0.6, p=0.24, not significant**), so a 4–5 label router sits in the unfavourable regime — except that **there is no training data**, and the alternatives collapse without it (**sentence-transformer + kNN: 28.1 on ATIS and 64.2 on CLINC150 at 5-shot**, versus 86.2 / 75.1 with full data). With 90 total labelled questions, a no-tuning rule, and the fact that an embedding-centroid router built from the project's own eval questions would be a direct tuning violation, **zero-shot with authored label definitions and authored few-shot exemplars is the only compatible option** — and few-shot is the cheapest non-fitting accuracy lever available (+10.9 points from 0-shot to 3-shot on one intent task, [arXiv 2608.02415](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02415v1)).

Three findings then redirect the design away from "better prediction" altogether. The **cascade/escalation** finding is the most important: existing adaptive-RAG systems choose a retrieval path before retrieval using only the query, and "the need for LLM augmentation is only revealed after searching the index" — comparing four machine-learning paradigms confirmed that **pre-retrieval routing cannot close the gap**, while a cheapest-first post-retrieval cascade that escalates when a step returns nothing beat always-heavy retrieval by **+0.140 composite with latency −31.8%**, serving **72.2% of real queries without augmentation**, with no training overhead ([The Coverage Illusion, arXiv 2605.27220v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27220v1)). Applied here: instead of predicting from the question whether to admit `#context` or retired units, search cheaply first and escalate only when the cheap pass returns nothing. That **converts a 21%-precision prediction problem into a zero-precision-required fallback**, needs no labelled data and no threshold, and is ratchet-safe because the cheap path is the current behaviour. It is the mechanism most likely to close G-058. The same paper carries a warning for the project's own numbers: synthetic queries suggested >90% of queries needed augmentation where only **27.8% of real user queries** did, because synthetic queries are more verbose and grammatically complete — so if the 90 questions were authored rather than harvested, measured router precision is optimistic about verbose questions and pessimistic about terse ones.

The **independent-booleans** finding fixes the shape: the router should return `needs_context_section: bool`, `admits_retired_units: bool`, `document: enum|null`, not one multi-class label. Zero-shot LLMs on compound intents "return plausible but non-canonical label combinations" and few-shot LLMs "tend to output only some of the gold intents" ([arXiv 2608.20371](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.20371); [arXiv 2510.07776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.07776)), and on genuinely unseen combinations whole-utterance baselines score **0.0 exact match where a clause-factorised decoder retains 91.1** ([arXiv 2603.28929](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.28929)). "Why was the withdrawn clause removed" is simultaneously WHY and HISTORY; required JSON keys on independent axes make the silent omission impossible. And **abstention must be threshold-free**: an explicit `NONE` label the model is permitted to emit, not a confidence cut, because every published abstention threshold is a held-out fit and verbalised confidence is structurally too sparse to cut finely — Qwen3-32B emits only **eight distinct confidence values on SST-2, more than half of them exactly 95%**, with 45–93% of values falling in the five most common ([arXiv 2608.04899](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.04899)). Two operational notes: a reasoning-distilled model under a tight output budget scores near zero because `<think>` traces consume it (**DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B: 0.002**, a token-budget artefact), so thinking must be disabled and **unparseable output must count as NONE rather than as a route**; and the only sub-decision where hard commitment is defensible at all is the word→filename table, because it is a genuine partition with a verifiable key — which is also the table whose two worst rows are fixed not by a classifier but by the alias row the rename needs.

## What must be reported for the result to be honest

The metric set that would be hard to criticise follows the literature's own pairing advice — a shallow task-aligned cutoff alongside a recall ceiling, since recall-based metrics are the family most sensitive to depth and depths are usually chosen "without necessarily fully considering the ramifications" ([Lu, Moffat & Culpepper](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/abstracts/lmc16irj.pdf)). **@4 is defensible and should be defended in one sentence**: the reader receives four passages, so Hit@4 is the probability the reader can answer, the cutoff was fixed by the context budget before measuring, and @10 would measure a system nobody runs. Around it: **graded recall@4 (the fraction of expected passages retrieved) as the primary continuous measure, in place of all-evidence@4 as a headline**, because all-evidence@4 is strict recall binarised at 1.0 and carries five pathologies — cardinality confounding (one expected passage needs one of four slots, three need three), permanent zeros for any question with more than four expected passages, non-monotonicity because a new correct passage can evict another expected one, maximum coarseness giving it strictly lower power than Hit@4, and label-completeness sensitivity, where a system that finds a *better* passage than the qrels name is scored as a failure and forcing the labelled passage into the top four is measured as a gain while being a reader regression. **Those five pathologies are derived from the metric's definition, not cited** — there is no published analysis of strict-recall@k at all, which is itself a finding. Then **rank-of-first-expected and max-rank-of-expected as continuous companions**, because they move when the system genuinely improves even when a step function does not, cannot be gamed by a threshold, and turn "three questions failed" into "three questions whose evidence sits at ranks 5, 6 and 11". Then **Temporal Coverage@k** — do the retrieved units' validity intervals actually cover the window endpoints — which is computable from the bitemporal model with no annotation and penalises exactly the born-inside-the-window narration distractor; it comes from [TEMPO (EMNLP 2026)](https://aclanthology.org/2026.emnlp-main.291.pdf) and the note that found it flags that the definition was read from a snippet and should be treated as **a lead, not a verified definition**. And finally **candidate-set recall reported as a set property with no ranking involved**, following TREC Temporal Summarization's deliberate decomposition of filtering from summarisation ([TREC 2015 overview](https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec24/papers/Overview-TS.pdf)): "9,537→42 with the expected address retained, 5/5" is a legitimate low-variance claim, while "Hit@4 1/5→2/5" is one rank flip at ±20 points per question and is not.

Per mechanism, four numbers over all 90 questions: **fired-and-helped, fired-and-harmed, fired-and-no-op, did-not-fire** — and the diagnostic inverts the usual intuition, because a mechanism that fires on exactly the three broken questions has firing precision 3/3 and is almost certainly a lookup table, while one that fires on 20, helps 3, harms 0 and no-ops 17 is a plausible general rule. **High precision on the eval set is the suspicious outcome, not the reassuring one.** Alongside it, an **off-set firing rate** over 50–200 unlabelled queries (restated questions, questions built from corpus headings, synthetic identifier queries): a rule meant to catch as-of temporal queries that fires on 2% of them is keyed to the eval phrasings, and measuring this needs no qrels at all. For any router, **its precision and recall as a classifier, reported separately from the end-to-end gain**, because a router with 41% routing precision that nonetheless gains three questions is riding luck and a router with 95% precision that gains three questions is a working component whose benefit on this corpus is small — completely different engineering situations that the retrieval metric cannot tell apart. This checklist is **constructed, not cited**: no established test or criterion exists in the literature for "mechanism or fit" at small n.

The rest is disclosure. Strata reported as **k/n counts, never percentages** — "2/3" is honest, "67%" implies a precision that does not exist — and reported as a per-question matrix rather than a scoreboard, since under Bonferroni across 12 strata the required **9 discordant questions exceeds the largest stratum (7), so no per-stratum claim can be significant even if every question in it flips**. The **look count** per set per episode, which is the number that lets a reader discount the result and the number nobody publishes. The **rejected candidates**, because a series reporting only accepted changes is reporting the maximum of a noisy process. The **chunk-leakage check** — the qrels name expected passages, so any change to chunking, section seaming or the collection split silently changes what the metric means, and a frozen set is only frozen if its passage ids are stable. The **PRIMAD-style environment record**, which the project already half-keeps by pinning seed, top_p, top_k and keep_alive; that habit maps onto PRIMAD's Platform and Implementation axes and is what makes a "different team, same setup" replication possible ([ir_metadata](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08922v1)). And a standing **non-claims paragraph**: this set cannot distinguish 100% from 94%, no per-stratum result can be significant, a three-question change is p=0.250, and the ratchet guarantees monotonicity rather than evidencing improvement.

The absences are load-bearing and should be published as findings rather than quietly omitted. There is **no controlled hard-filter versus soft-boost study** — the nearest thing is Google's context-dependent default (hard filters for single-datastore search, soft boosts for multi-datastore), which is a design choice with no published evaluation ([Google Cloud](https://docs.cloud.google.com/python/docs/reference/discoveryengine/latest/google.cloud.discoveryengine_v1.types.SearchRequest.NaturalLanguageQueryUnderstandingSpec.ExtractedFilterBehavior)). There is **no prior art on learning or deriving an intent→section mapping from a schema**, as paper or production write-up; the search returned curriculum-mapping literature. There is **no published text-to-filter accuracy figure for any model** — AWS, NVIDIA, Haystack and Flowise all ship the feature and none reports accuracy. There is **no published engineering write-up of a search system that accepts a free-text "what changed between X and Y" query** over a versioned corpus: every legal system found requires the user to have already identified the provision through a UI control, so if the project's contribution is doing that identification from the question, **there is no baseline to beat**. There is **no analysis of strict-recall@k** as a metric. There is no evaluation of a churn-count-within-window predicate for retrieval candidate selection, no measured study of alias/redirect edges in search indexes, no measured section/field routing at tiny-eval scale, no empirical study of adaptivity-driven overfitting at n<100, and no published IR paper reporting exact binomial bounds for Hit@k at these sizes — the binomial treatment above is standard statistics applied to an IR setting, **not a cited IR convention**.

## The decisions that belong to the author

Six questions the research cannot settle. **Window semantics**: first-parent position is monotone and total along the mainline, which is what the predicate needs, but a merge collapses a branch's commits to the merge's single position — correct for "what was in force", wrong for "what did people change", and a choice to make explicitly rather than inherit. **Endpoint resolution under day granularity**: resolving the window start to the day's last position matches legal point-in-time practice (*as at end of* a date) and resolving it to the first position silently pulls in same-day changes, so the convention should be declared and the resolved positions echoed back in the answer. **The repeal arm**: add the third branch of the insertion/repeal/substitution taxonomy with no question that can verify it, or leave the hole and declare it — but not leave it undeclared, since `repealed` is 0 in all five windows and nothing in the set would warn us. **Whether the time set's pre-resolved tag windows stay pre-resolved**, which decides whether 24/24 describes the system or a slightly easier task. **Whether to accept the residual**: 45/47 with the two ranking losses published and explained, against 47/47 bought with a tweak whose firing set is the fault's firing set. And **whether to grow the set**, since 15 questions an episode reaches the 150-topic power threshold in about seven episodes and a frozen 90 never does — which is the difference between a series that can eventually claim an improvement and one that can only ever claim mechanisms.

## Conclusion

The deepest finding is that this project's two hardest constraints point in opposite directions and only one of them can be satisfied. The no-tuning rule and the ratchet are exactly the right instincts — procedurally, the project has independently reinvented the Ladder's submission limit and MS MARCO's private leaderboard by scoring the held-out 13 once per episode — but they guarantee a monotone curve that carries no evidential weight, while the eight-question gap is too small to produce a significant result unless it is spent all at once. The resolution is not to find better mechanisms for the five stubborn questions; it is to stop treating the headline proportion as the deliverable. **The per-question matrix is the result and the aggregates are a summary of it**, because at n=90 the matrix is the only artefact a reader can audit, and the rank-of-first-expected column is the one that distinguishes a near miss at rank 5 from a retrieval failure at rank 25 — a distinction Hit@4 structurally cannot make and the one the blog series actually has something to say about.

There is also a specific intellectual asset here that the literature does not have. The project's own measurement — candidate restriction with the reader held fixed, recall verified as a set property, `born-once` quantified at 4–10× the moved set — is **closer to a clean ablation of version-awareness versus diff-indexing than anything published**, since VersionRAG's 25–30%→70% change-query gain conflates the two and compares only against version-unaware baselines ([arXiv:2510.08109](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08109v1)). And the divergence is real rather than incidental: VersionRAG treats the changelog narrating a change as the easy, desirable retrieval target, whereas in this corpus that narration is precisely the distractor and the expected answer is the requirement text itself. A write-up that leads with the mechanism, the verified-recall restriction, the firing precision of the three incumbent lists, and the two questions it honestly closes will outlast one that leads with a number the set cannot support.
